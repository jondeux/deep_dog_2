## Final Report

# Time-Restricted Eating and Intermittent Fasting vs Continuous Calorie Restriction: How Strong Is the Evidence for Long-Term Cardiometabolic Health in Adults? (Evidence to November 2026)

## Executive Summary

The evidence supports time-restricted eating (TRE) and intermittent fasting (IF) as a reasonable alternative to continuous calorie restriction (CCR). It does not support them as superior to CCR, and it cannot establish long-term cardiovascular benefit. The best 12-month trials found no statistically significant difference from CCR in weight: Liu 2022 (TRE added to CCR; net −1.8 kg, 95% CI −4.0 to 0.4) [1], Lin 2023 (TRE vs CCR 0.81 kg, −3.07 to 4.69) [2] and Trepanowski 2017 (ADF vs CCR) [3]. The largest network meta-analysis (BMJ 2025; 99 RCTs, 6,582 adults) found that only alternate-day fasting beat CCR on weight, by 1.29 kg (−1.99 to −0.59, moderate certainty). It found no HbA1c differences, and only 17 trials lasted 24 weeks or longer [4].

Blood pressure and glycaemic gains are small. They are inconsistent across reviews (compare [5] with [6]) and are hard to separate from the energy deficit. The best isocaloric trial found no timing effect (0.3 kg, −1.2 to 1.9) [7]. However, a 2025 crossover trial showed that "isocaloric" designs still leak a deficit [8]. The early-eating-window signal remains small-trial evidence [9][10].

No randomised trial has reported cardiovascular events or mortality. The Cochrane review found no such data [11], and I found no registered hard-endpoint trial. Observational data are discordant: a NHANES analysis links eating windows under 8 hours to higher cardiovascular mortality (HR 2.35, 1.39–3.98) [12], while a 2026 meta-analysis of observational studies found null cross-sectional associations [13]. Overall certainty is low to moderate for weight and low or very low for blood pressure, glycaemia and cardiovascular outcomes. Several prominent advocates are also investigators of the trials in this field.

## Scope, Definitions and Comparator Taxonomy

Whether TRE or IF "beats" CCR depends on what the comparator was, and reviews mix several designs. Four comparator types appear in the evidence. The first is true CCR, in which the control group receives a prescribed daily deficit. Examples are the 25% reduction in Lin et al. [2] and the 1500–1800 kcal/day (men) and 1200–1500 kcal/day (women) prescription in Liu et al. [1]. The second is TRE added on top of CCR versus CCR alone, which tests timing as an add-on [1]. The third is an unrestricted or usual-eating control, which cannot show superiority over CCR. Examples are the control of 10 or more eating hours per day in Lin et al. [2] and the unrestricted arm in Oldenburg et al. [14]. The fourth is an active non-CCR comparator, such as consistent three-meal timing in Lowe et al. [15], dietitian guidance in the Australian T2D trial [16], or standard-of-care counselling in Manoogian et al. [17].

Protocol definitions also vary. TRE confines eating to roughly 4–10 hours a day, with 8-hour (16:8) windows most common [18][19]. Alternate-day fasting (ADF) alternates fast days with feast days. In the modified version (mADF), fast days allow about 25% of energy needs [18][20]. The 5:2 diet restricts intake to roughly 500–600 kcal on two days a week [18][21]. This report treats 12 months or longer as "long-term". Most evidence is 12 weeks to 6 months.

Several older anchors predate the past two years and should be read as such: Trepanowski 2017 [3], Headland 2018 [22], Sundfør 2018 [23], Carter 2018 [24], Lowe 2020 [15] and Liu 2022 [1]. The 2021 umbrella review is by Chandran et al., not Patikorn as in the outline [25].

## Key Randomised Trials: Time-Restricted Eating

### Long-term trials (12 months)

Liu et al. (NEJM 2022) randomised 139 adults with obesity in Guangzhou, China, to 8-hour TRE (08:00–16:00) plus calorie restriction or calorie restriction alone for 12 months. 118 (84.9%) completed [1]. Weight fell 8.0 kg (−9.6 to −6.4) with TRE plus CCR and 6.3 kg (−7.8 to −4.7) with CCR alone. The net difference was −1.8 kg (−4.0 to 0.4; P=0.11). Waist, BMI, body fat, lean mass, blood pressure and metabolic risk factors were consistent with this, and adverse events were similar [1]. Both arms were calorie-restricted, so this tests timing as an add-on, not TRE as an alternative to CCR. Observers were blinded to assignment, but participants could not be [1].

Lin et al. (Annals of Internal Medicine 2023) is the only 12-month trial in the evidence base to test TRE without calorie counting against CCR. Ninety adults with obesity in Chicago (33% Black, 46% Hispanic) were randomised to 8-hour TRE (noon–8 p.m.), 25% CCR, or control. Seventy-seven completed [2]. Compared with control, weight loss at 12 months was −4.61 kg (−7.37 to −1.85) for TRE and −5.42 kg (−9.13 to −1.71) for CCR. TRE vs CCR was 0.81 kg (−3.07 to 4.69; P=0.68). Energy intake fell by about 425 kcal/day with TRE and 405 kcal/day with CCR [2]. Authors listed the limitations as lack of blinding, being underpowered for large differences, and no multiplicity adjustment [2]. An accompanying editorial argued that TRE works with concurrent nutrition counselling and that patient preference should guide choice (single secondary report) [26]. The CI in Lin is wide enough to include a 3–4 kg advantage for either strategy.

### Medium- and short-term trials

Lowe et al. (JAMA Internal Medicine 2020, TREAT) randomised 116 adults (BMI 27–43) to 16:8 TRE (ad libitum 12–8 p.m.) or consistent meal timing for 12 weeks [15][27]. Weight change was −0.94 kg with TRE and −0.68 kg with consistent meal timing. The between-group difference was −0.26 kg (−1.30 to 0.78; P=0.63) [27]. The authors concluded that TRE conferred no weight loss or cardiometabolic benefit [15]. A published letter reported an appendicular lean mass reduction (between-group −0.47 kg, −0.82 to −0.12, about 1.8%). It argued this was close to measurement error and that the active comparator, breakfast skipping and Hawthorne effects complicated interpretation. The letter's authors disclosed consultancy and research ties to IF work [28]. The senior author disclosed equity in Keyto and Virta [15].

Maruthur et al. (Annals 2024) is the strongest test of timing independent of energy. Forty-one adults with obesity and prediabetes or diet-controlled diabetes (93% women, 93% Black) were fed isocalorically in a clinical research unit for 12 weeks. Eating was either a 10-hour TRE window with 80% of calories before 1 p.m., or a usual pattern (window of 16 hours or less, at least 50% of calories after 5 p.m.) [7][29]. Weight fell 2.3 kg (1.0 to 3.5) with TRE and 2.6 kg (1.5 to 3.7) with the usual pattern. The difference was 0.3 kg (−1.2 to 1.9), and fasting glucose, HOMA-IR and glycated albumin did not differ [7]. The authors noted the study was small and single-site, with baseline weight imbalance (95.6 vs 103.7 kg). They concluded that effects in prior TRE studies may be due to reduced calorie intake [7]. Both arms lost weight despite the isocaloric design, so the feeding protocol was not perfectly energy-neutral [29].

Oldenburg et al. (Obesity 2025) randomised 88 adults with BMI 30–55 to self-selected 8-hour TRE, 15% CCR, or unrestricted eating for 12 weeks; 81 (92%) completed. Final windows were 9.8, 12.9 and 11.8 hours [14][30]. Versus unrestricted eating, weight change was −1.4 kg (−4.5 to 1.7) with TRE and −2.5 kg (−5.8 to 0.8) with CCR. Neither differed significantly, and body composition, HbA1c, clamp and CGM measures were similar. TRE had lower metabolic flexibility than CCR (−0.041; −0.080 to −0.002). Shrinking the eating window correlated with lower caloric intake and visceral fat [14]. The trial is underpowered for a TRE-vs-CCR comparison. Its authors include investigators who also run other TRE trials [30].

Črešnovar et al. (Clinical Nutrition 2025) randomised 108 adults with overweight or obesity, all energy-restricted, to early 8-hour TRE, late 8-hour TRE or 12-hour eating for 3 months [9]. Body mass fell about 4–5 kg in every arm with no difference (P=0.319). Early TRE beat late TRE for fasting glucose (−0.35 mmol/L, −0.63 to −0.06) and fat mass (−1.2%). It beat energy restriction alone for diastolic pressure (−4 mmHg, −8 to 0, interval touching zero) and metabolic age [9]. The analysis was per-protocol, allocation was by chronotype, and 93 of 108 completed [9].

Peters et al. (Science Translational Medicine, October 2025) ran a 2-week crossover of early (08:00–16:00) and late (13:00–21:00) TRE in 31 women with overweight or obesity, who were asked to keep habitual intake. Insulin sensitivity did not differ (−0.07; −0.77 to 0.62) [8]. Early TRE nevertheless produced a deficit of about 167 kcal/day and 1.08 kg weight loss. This shows how "intended isocaloric" TRE can still contain a calorie reduction [8].

### Special populations

In metabolic syndrome, Manoogian et al. (Annals 2024) found personalised 8–10-hour TRE plus standard counselling improved HbA1c by −0.10% (−0.19 to −0.003) over counselling alone at 3 months, with 108 completers and no major adverse events [17]. The comparator was not calorie-matched. In T2D, Parr et al. (2024) randomised 51 adults to TRE (10:00–19:00) or dietitian-led guidance for 6 months. HbA1c fell 0.4% vs 0.3%, and TRE was non-inferior (−0.11%; −0.50 to 0.28 against a 0.3% margin). Both arms lost weight through about 900 kJ/day spontaneous energy reduction [16]. In MASLD, the CHRONO-NAFLD trial found all three arms, including an untimed Mediterranean-diet control, lost weight and lowered blood pressure. Only early TRE improved HbA1c and insulin-resistance indices (59 completers; between-group statistics not verified here) [31]. TRE protocols in these populations also reduced energy intake [16], which weakens attribution to timing.

## Key Randomised Trials: Intermittent Fasting (ADF, 5:2, Periodic)

Trepanowski et al. (JAMA Internal Medicine 2017; 2017 publication) is the only 12-month ADF-vs-CCR-vs-control RCT in the evidence set [20]. One hundred metabolically healthy adults with obesity (63% Black) were assigned to ADF (25% of energy needs on fast days, 125% on feast days), 75% daily CCR, or control, with 6 months of weight loss then 6 months of maintenance [20][32]. Dropout was 38% with ADF, 29% with CCR and 26% with control [3]. Weight change at month 12 was −6.0% (−8.5 to −3.6) for ADF and −5.3% (−7.6 to −3.0) for CCR. Blood pressure, glucose, insulin and HOMA-IR did not differ between arms, and LDL cholesterol was 11.5 mg/dL higher with ADF at month 12 (1.9 to 21.1) [3]. ADF participants ate more than prescribed on fast days and less on feast days, while the CCR group generally met its target [20]. The authors concluded ADF produced no superior adherence, weight loss, maintenance or cardioprotection [3]. The abstract describes weight figures "relative to control" while a figure caption describes change from baseline, so the reference point is ambiguous. The between-group effect table (Table 2) could not be retrieved, so the pairwise estimates beyond the abstract are not verified.

Headland et al. (International Journal of Obesity 2018) randomised 332 adults to CCR, week-on/week-off restriction or 5:2. Only 146 (44%) completed, a 56% attrition rate. Completer weight losses were −6.6, −5.1 and −5.0 kg (time-by-diet P=0.2), with similar HDL and triglyceride improvements and no difference in discontinuation (P=0.4) [22]. The prescriptions differed: CCR 4,200 kJ/day for women, versus 2,100 kJ on two days a week for 5:2 [22]. A follow-up of 109 participants at 24 months showed −4.5, −2.8 and −3.5 kg with no between-diet differences. The abstract reports no confidence intervals, so precision cannot be judged [33]. Both Headland reports are completer-based, so attrition may bias them.

Sundfør et al. (Nutrition, Metabolism and Cardiovascular Diseases 2018) randomised 112 adults with abdominal obesity to 5:2-type restriction (400/600 kcal on two non-consecutive days) or continuous restriction [23]. Dietary records showed both arms cut intake by about 26–28%, so the measured deficit was approximately matched. Weight loss was 8.0 vs 9.0 kg (P=0.6), and regain after the unsupervised maintenance phase was minimal (1.1 vs 0.4 kg). Hunger scores were higher with IF (4.7 vs 3.6; P=0.002) [23]. The authors suggested hunger may limit long-term adherence to intermittent restriction [23].

Carter et al. (JAMA Network Open 2018) is the only 12-month non-inferiority trial in T2D. 137 adults were randomised to IF (500–600 kcal on 2 non-consecutive days) or CCR (1,200–1,500 kcal/day); 97 completed [24]. HbA1c fell 0.3% vs 0.5%, a difference of 0.2% (90% CI −0.2 to 0.5), which met the ±0.5% equivalence margin. Weight fell 6.8 vs 5.0 kg, and the −1.8 kg difference (90% CI −3.7 to 0.07) did not meet the ±2.5 kg equivalence margin. Equivalence for fat mass and fat-free mass was likewise not shown [24]. Hypoglycaemic or hyperglycaemic events occurred in the first two weeks in 35% (16/46) of sulfonylurea and/or insulin users, with medications reduced at baseline per protocol [24]. The 24-month follow-up was not retrieved.

A 2024 Chinese 5:2-vs-CCR trial in 60 adults with MAFLD found no difference in weight, lipids or glycaemia at 12 weeks. The 5:2 arm had lower steatosis and fibrosis proportions (fibrosis P=0.05; no confidence intervals) [34]. It is short, imaging-based and single-centre. I found no 2025–2026 IF trial of 12 months or longer.

## Meta-Analyses, Network Meta-Analyses and Umbrella Reviews

### Network meta-analysis and Cochrane

Semnani-Azad et al. (BMJ 2025) is the highest-quality review for the question. The authors pooled 99 RCTs (6,582 adults; 720 healthy and 5,862 with existing conditions) with searches to November 2024. They used a frequentist network meta-analysis and GRADE [4][35]. All IF and CCR strategies reduced weight against ad libitum eating. Versus CCR, only ADF showed a benefit (−1.29 kg; −1.99 to −0.59; moderate certainty). ADF also beat TRE (−1.69 kg) and whole-day fasting (−1.05 kg) [4]. The ADF advantage appeared in trials shorter than 24 weeks (n=76), while the 17 trials of 24 weeks or longer showed weight benefit only against ad libitum eating [4]. HbA1c and HDL did not differ between IF, CCR and ad libitum diets. TRE raised total cholesterol, LDL and non-HDL cholesterol compared with whole-day fasting [4]. The authors concluded that IF gives similar benefits to CCR and that longer trials are needed. A later correction notice concerned an author affiliation and did not change results [36]. The TRE-vs-CCR point estimate was not retrievable in the abstract read. The network mixes energy-matched and unmatched TRE trials [35].

The Cochrane review (CD013496; search to December 2019) pooled 18 RCTs (1,125 adults, 4 weeks to 6 months) [11][37]. It found no data on all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, stroke, myocardial infarction or heart failure. IF beat ad libitum eating for weight in the short term (−2.88 kg; −3.96 to −1.80; low certainty). Against CCR it was uncertain in the short term (−0.88 kg; −1.76 to 0.00; very low certainty) and showed no effect in the medium term (−0.56 kg; −1.68 to 0.56; low certainty). Glucose did not differ, and changes were judged not clinically significant [11]. This is the main independent systematic-review benchmark but is seven years old by search date.

### Umbrella reviews

Chandran et al. (JAMA Network Open, December 2021; search to January 2021) mapped 11 meta-analyses and shows the comparator mixing directly. Its Table 1 lists reviews comparing IF with regular diet, CCR or both, with AMSTAR-2 ratings mostly low or moderate [25]. Its GRADE ratings ranged from high (BMI −1.20 vs regular diet, 4 studies, I² 0%) to low or very low for other BMI estimates. Body weight for modified ADF vs CCR was −1.65 kg (−2.73 to −0.58; moderate; 2 studies) [25]. This shows that the grade is driven by comparator and study count. Sun et al. (eClinicalMedicine 2024) re-analysed 351 associations from 23 meta-analyses across 34 outcomes, searched to January 2024, using AMSTAR and GRADE [38]. Hua et al. (Diabetes, Obesity and Metabolism, December 2024) is another 2024 umbrella review with overlapping scope [18]. For both, pooled estimates and GRADE distributions were not extracted here.

### Why reviews diverge

Three mechanisms explain most of the divergence. The first is comparator mixing. Kibret et al. compared with usual diet and graded effects "high certainty" [43]. The BMJ network meta-analysis compared against CCR and graded the ADF-vs-CCR weight difference moderate, with a much smaller magnitude [4]. The second is whether "isocaloric" truly means energy-matched. Fernandes-Alves reports benefit at isocaloric control [40], but the abstract does not show how isocaloric was defined [45]. The third is protocol and population heterogeneity: Yi pooled non-CR TRE in non-diabetic adults [5], Nam only crossover RCTs [44], and Lu et al. mixed IF with very-low-calorie regimens [46].

Sun et al. conclude that adding TRE to energy restriction gives no further weight benefit, yet their own subgroup estimate (−0.94 kg, CI excluding zero) points to a small add-on [41]. Črešnovar's pooled add-on is larger (−2.11 kg) [42]. The individual add-on trials (Liu, CIs −4.0 to 0.4) are underpowered. A 1–2 kg add-on cannot be excluded, but it also cannot be shown to be timing-specific.

## Findings by Outcome

### Body weight and adiposity

Against ad libitum eating, TRE and IF produce modest weight loss: about −1.4 to −1.6 kg for TRE in several reviews [39][41][47]. Against CCR, the 12-month trials and the BMJ network meta-analysis show similar loss, with ADF perhaps 1.3 kg better in short trials and no benefit in trials of 24 weeks or longer [4]. Lin's TRE-vs-CCR difference was 0.81 kg [2]. Headland's IF arms lost 5.0–5.1 kg against 6.6 kg for CCR, not significantly different [22]. The 24-month follow-up showed convergence and no differences [33]. Meta-analyses of TRE in women find −1.93 kg overall, with no significant difference against CCR (P=0.295) [48]. Lean mass loss is reported inconsistently. Fat-free mass fell by about 0.4–0.6 kg in meta-analyses [40][41] and appendicular lean mass fell in TREAT [28]. Meanwhile the women-only review found no lean mass loss [48]. Weight maintenance beyond 12 months rests on Headland's 24-month follow-up and Sundfør's maintenance phase (regain 1.1 vs 0.4 kg) [33][23].

### Blood pressure

Evidence on blood pressure is inconsistent. Yi et al. pooled 11 non-CR TRE trials and found SBP −1.79 mmHg (−3.30 to −0.27) and DBP −1.75 mmHg [5]. Qi et al. pooled 22 trials and found no blood pressure difference [6]. A mini-review calls a lowering of about 4/2 mmHg the "most consistent finding" while saying robust benefit is limited and that benefit against CCR is not consistent [19]. Kibret et al. report larger effects against usual diet (mADF SBP −7.24 mmHg), but these rest on a comparator with no active weight-loss component [43]. In trials, ADF did not differ from CCR for blood pressure at 12 months [3], and blood pressure improved similarly in both arms of Sundfør [23] and across all arms of CHRONO-NAFLD, including the untimed control [31]. These patterns suggest blood pressure tracks weight loss. The only signal of a timing effect is Črešnovar's early TRE DBP −4 mmHg versus energy restriction, which has a CI touching zero [9]. Meta-analytic authors say effects are larger in people with elevated baseline blood pressure, but that subgroup finding needs full-text checking [5].

### Glycaemic control and insulin sensitivity

At the pooled level, HbA1c does not differ between IF, CCR and ad libitum eating in the BMJ network meta-analysis [4]. In T2D, trials are consistent with equivalence to CCR: Carter's HbA1c difference of 0.2% (90% CI −0.2 to 0.5) met equivalence [24], and Parr found TRE non-inferior to dietitian guidance [16]. A TRE meta-analysis in T2D found HbA1c −0.11% (−0.15 to −0.07), but it was limited to crossover RCTs [44], and the metabolic-syndrome TRE trial found −0.10% [17]. These HbA1c changes are small. Lu et al. found HbA1c −0.08 points with HOMA-IR −0.60, in a pool mixing IF with very-low-calorie regimens [46]. The Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology Personal View cites HbA1c reductions of typically 0.3–1.2% against usual eating in T2D [49]. This is an advocacy piece by trialists, not a systematic review. Hypoglycaemia risk was addressed in Carter with medication reduction [24], and the Personal View states that IF does not increase hypoglycaemia risk when medications are adjusted using simple rules [49]. Insulin-sensitivity benefits appear mostly for early TRE (Hamsho: fasting glucose −3.64 mg/dL; HOMA-IR −0.42, with CrI close to zero) [10]. The isocaloric trials found none [7][8].

### Lipids and other markers

Lipid effects are mostly null or adverse for IF relative to CCR. ADF raised LDL by 11.5 mg/dL over CCR at 12 months [3]. TRE raised total and LDL cholesterol compared with whole-day fasting in the BMJ network meta-analysis [4]. Yi et al. found no meaningful lipid effect without caloric restriction [5]. Panagiotou et al. found small decreases (LDL −2.7 mg/dL) against regular diet [47].

### Cardiovascular events and mortality

No RCT of TRE or IF in the evidence set reports cardiovascular events or mortality. The Cochrane review pre-specified these outcomes and none of the 18 included studies reported them [11]. A 2025 observational analysis states that long-term hard-endpoint RCTs are unavailable [12]. The 2026 ACC Scientific Statement acknowledges that hard-endpoint nutrition RCTs are "often infeasible" and bases recommendations on the totality of evidence [50]. The 2026 TRE overweight-and-obesity evidence summary calls for future research on hard clinical endpoints [51]. Registry searches found only small trials: TRES (acute coronary syndrome, n=48, 4 weeks, weight primary outcome) [52] and NY-TREAT (n=57, 12 months, completed April 2026, fat mass, energy intake, weight and HOMA-IR as primary outcomes; no results verified) [53]. I found no registered trial with events as a primary endpoint. That is "searched and not found" in ClinicalTrials.gov results, not proof of absence.

## Robustness of the Evidence

Risk of bias is substantial. In Liu et al.'s meta-analysis, 22 of 29 trials had high risk-of-bias concerns and 7 had some concerns [39]. Participants cannot be blinded. Lin's trial states this [2], and only Liu's trial reports blinded observers [1]. Wang's 5:2 trial is described as double-blind [34], although how that was achieved is unclear.

Duration is the key constraint. Most trials last 12 weeks to 6 months [39][11]. In the BMJ network meta-analysis, 76 trials were under 24 weeks and 17 were 24 weeks or longer [4]. Attrition is high in long trials: 38% in the ADF arm of Trepanowski [3], 56% in Headland [22], 29% in Carter [24] and about 15% in Liu [1]. Completer analyses can overstate durable effects, and Headland's report is completer-based.

Energy-intake measurement is weak. Most TRE trials use self-reported 24-hour recalls, which are "notoriously inaccurate" [54]. Oldenburg used 24-hour recall [14], while Lin reported intake reductions by an unspecified method [2]. Only NY-TREAT registers doubly labelled water [53]. Adherence is mostly self-reported: TREAT used daily yes/no surveys [27] and Parr used self-report [16]. The Chang et al.-type narrative reviews and Søndergaard note that RESET adherence was 91% but participants returned to baseline eating afterward [19].

Certainty ratings conflict. The BMJ network meta-analysis grades the key ADF-vs-CCR weight comparison moderate [4], Hamsho grades very low to moderate [10], and Kibret labels results high certainty versus usual diet [43]. The Cochrane ratings range from very low to low [11]. Publication bias is rarely assessed beyond underpowered tests (for example Begg's test with 8 studies in [46]). I did not find funnel-plot or registry-based publication-bias analyses.

Research-group clustering is visible but not quantified. Varady is an author of Lin [2], Trepanowski [20] and Hamsho [10], of the Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology Personal View [49], and of the 2017 AHA statement [55]. Chow and Varady are authors on Oldenburg [30]. Peterson co-authored Maruthur and the Personal View [7][49]. The conflict-of-interest and funding statements for the Personal View were not retrieved [21]. These are overlapping research networks, not evidence of bias, but independent replication matters.

## Weight Loss and Energy Intake vs Timing of Eating

The central dispute is whether benefits come from timing itself or from the energy deficit that timing induces. Evidence for the deficit reading is strong. In Lin, TRE cut intake by about 425 kcal/day, comparable to CCR at 405 kcal/day, with similar weight loss [2]. Parr's T2D trial found about 900 kJ/day spontaneous energy reduction [16]. A Nutrients review calls free-living improvements "primarily driven by spontaneous caloric restriction" and says isocaloric RCTs "generally show no additional benefits" [56]. Panagiotou et al. cite roughly 350–500 kcal/day lower intake with TRE [47]. The Oldenburg correlation (smaller eating window, lower intake) supports an intake-mediated pathway [14]. Maruthur's isocaloric feeding study found no timing effect on weight or glycaemia [7].

The case for a timing effect rests on Fernandes-Alves (isocaloric −1.46 kg, interpreted as circadian enhancement of calorie restriction) [40], early TRE signals [9][31][10], and a cardiovascular-physiology review proposing circadian alignment [57]. Yi et al. argue that benefits in non-CR TRE trials reflect feeding pattern [5], although energy intake was not controlled in those trials [58].

These positions are less contradictory than they look. Maruthur's CI (−1.2 to 1.9 kg) is compatible with the pooled isocaloric estimate [29]. Peters shows that "intended isocaloric" trials still carry unintended deficits (−167 kcal/day, −1.08 kg) [8], so Fernandes-Alves's isocaloric pool may include residual energy differences [45]. The methodologically cleanest design (fixed-intake feeding) found no weight effect, but it is small (n=41), short and in a population that is 93% Black women [7]. Assessing early-versus-late timing, a mini-review notes that only a few small, short RCTs compare early with late TRE and that evidence for circadian effects is "lacking" [19]. Mechanisms such as ketogenesis, autophagy and microbiome effects were not substantiated with human trial data in the sources reviewed; animal-versus-human evidence for them was not assessed.

## Subgroup and Protocol Differences

By protocol, ADF showed a short-term weight advantage over CCR and TRE in the BMJ network meta-analysis [4]. TRE trials suggest early windows may confer a modest glycaemic edge over late windows [9][10], but several early-versus-late comparisons are 2–12 weeks long [8][9]. 5:2 trials show weight outcomes statistically similar to CCR [22][23], with higher hunger in one [23].

By population, T2D evidence is mostly glycaemic and shows equivalence or non-inferiority to CCR or dietitian care [24][16]. In metabolic syndrome, small HbA1c gains were seen against counselling alone [17]. In MASLD, liver outcomes are mixed and short [31][34]. In women (at least 85% female pooled), TRE is not superior to CCR for weight [48]. Trials include racially diverse samples (Lin: 33% Black and 46% Hispanic [2]; Maruthur: 93% Black [7]), but Liu is entirely Chinese [1]. Older adults, shift workers and people with established cardiovascular disease are barely represented in completed trials; NY-TREAT targets ages 50–75 but has no verified results [53]. Evidence on region is limited to the setting of individual trials.

## Safety and Observational Signals

The AHA 2024 abstract (P192) analysed 20,078 NHANES adults (2003–2018) with two 24-hour recalls and National Death Index follow-up (median 8.0 years). Eating durations under 8 hours (versus 12–16 hours) were associated with cardiovascular mortality (HR 1.96; 1.23–3.13) [59]. The signal was larger among those with cardiovascular disease (HR 2.06) or cancer (HR 2.72), and all-cause mortality showed no significant association [59]. It was later published in a peer-reviewed journal (Diabetes & Metabolic Syndrome 2025; read from a third-party-hosted PDF, so verify against the publisher). The reference group changed to 12–14 hours, and the HR for under 8 hours was 2.35 (1.39–3.98) [12]. The cardiovascular signal survived 14 sensitivity analyses and 8 subgroups, but the all-cause association did not. The authors conclude that further research is needed to tell whether the risk reflects short eating duration itself or residual confounding [12].

Critiques are specific. Attia and colleagues, working from the poster, noted the under-8-hour group had 414 participants and 31 deaths against 11,831 and 423 deaths, and was younger, with higher BMI and more smoking [60]. They said the share of cardiovascular deaths among people with pre-existing cardiovascular disease was about 38% in the short-window group vs 15% [60]. They also noted that a 30-minute misreport of first and last intake shifts the window by about an hour [60]. A science-journalism critique emphasised that two recalls cannot classify habitual TRE and that calorie intake was not adjusted for [61]. Both critiques predate the peer-reviewed version and are not peer-reviewed. Kenneth Mukamal said illness-driven eating patterns (for example cancer-related appetite loss) could explain apparent harm, and that there was no clear cardiovascular benefit either [62]. I found no peer-reviewed rebuttal or replication of this specific signal.

Other observational evidence points in different directions. Hu et al. (European Journal of Clinical Nutrition, July 2026; NHANES 1999–2018, 31,044 adults, 7,129 deaths) found later first meals (after 12:00) associated with higher all-cause mortality (HR 1.29; 1.10–1.51), as were last meals after midnight (1.27) or before 19:00 (1.13) [63]. The authors estimated 2.46 years shorter life expectancy at age 50 with a first meal after noon [63]. This sits awkwardly with common TRE schedules that start at noon (such as Lin and Lowe). That is my reading of the overlap, not a finding of the cohort. In contrast, Feit et al. (May 2026; 12 cross-sectional and 6 cohort studies) found null pooled cross-sectional odds ratios for abdominal obesity (0.96), metabolic syndrome (1.04) and elevated blood pressure (1.04), and prospective data were insufficient [13].

Trial-reported harms are modest and thinly reported. Hypoglycaemia was a concern in Carter among sulfonylurea and insulin users [24]. Higher hunger accompanied 5:2 [23]. Fat-free mass loss appears in meta-analyses [40][41] and lipid effects such as LDL rises with ADF [3]. Mild headaches were reported in four Cochrane-included studies [11]. No major adverse events were reported in the metabolic-syndrome TRE trial [17]. I found no systematic data on gallstones, bone density or eating-disorder risk. Guideline-style caution for insulin or sulfonylurea use, pregnancy and eating disorders was not verified against a guideline text in these sources. Randomised evidence (surrogate improvements, short term) and observational mortality associations (hard endpoint, high confounding risk) cannot be reconciled directly. The observational data cannot show harm from TRE, and the trials cannot show cardiovascular benefit.

## New and Conflicting Evidence (2025–November 2026)

Several 2025–2026 publications refine rather than overturn the picture. Oldenburg [14], Peters [8] and Maruthur [7] reinforce the energy-deficit reading, and Črešnovar [9] and Hamsho [10] keep an early-window hypothesis alive. The Hamsho network meta-analysis says differences between TRE patterns and calorie restriction were limited and rankings should be read cautiously [10]. Feit (May 2026) is a cautionary observational null [13], and Hu (July 2026) raises meal-timing mortality questions [63]. The January 2026 Liu et al. evidence summary states that some well-designed RCTs found no advantage of TRE over standard dietary advice [51]. Latimer et al. (August 2026) give a supportive mechanistic narrative that cites "significant benefits" while acknowledging limitations (not systematic) [57]. The BMJ network meta-analysis has an affiliation-only correction, not a retraction [36]. I found no retractions or expressions of concern.

On guidelines, Varady, Chow and Peterson (Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology, June 2026) argue IF should be added to diabetes guidelines. They state it "has not yet been recommended" in the ADA Standards of Care or IDF recommendations, and that the ADA in 2023 only briefly acknowledged mild-to-moderate weight loss [49][21]. The 2026 AHA/ACC/ADA/ASN cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic guideline retires the 2013 obesity guideline [64][65]. Its retrieved text and the ACC and Medscape summaries show no mention of IF or TRE, with nutrition framed generically [66][67]. The section on intensive lifestyle modification (5.4.2) was not read, so the guideline's IF position is unverified. The 2026 ACC nutrition statement emphasises overall dietary quality, and the retrieved portions do not single out TRE [50]. The 2017 AHA statement concluded that irregular eating patterns appear less favourable [55]. Mukamal's remark that food quality matters more than timing "at least as of now" reflects this orientation [62].

## Evidence Gaps and Weaknesses, Ranked

The most important gap is the absence of any hard-endpoint RCT, and the registry shows none planned that I could find [11][12][52][53]. Second is dependence on surrogate outcomes, with weight, HbA1c and blood pressure effects that are small (HbA1c −0.1%) [44][17]. Third is short duration, with 17 of 99 trials in the network meta-analysis lasting 24 weeks or longer [4]. Fourth is poor measurement of intake and adherence [54]. Fifth is population coverage: older adults, people with cardiovascular disease and many regions are under-represented [53]. Sixth is protocol heterogeneity and comparator mixing, which complicate pooling [25][43]. Seventh is overlapping research groups and advocacy, which warrants independent replication [49]. Items not found: gallstone, bone-density and eating-disorder trial data; Sun 2024 umbrella pooled estimates and GRADE distribution; Trepanowski Table 2 pairwise estimates; Carter's 24-month follow-up; guideline IF wording in the CKM 2026 guideline body; and any 2025–2026 IF RCT of 12 months or longer.

## Implications and Outlook

For a person with obesity, prediabetes or T2D who prefers fasting windows, TRE or 5:2 is a defensible alternative to CCR. The 12-month and equivalence trials show similar weight and HbA1c (Lin, Carter), but adherence should be monitored given high attrition and hunger in IF arms [2][24][23]. Evidence does not support claims that fasting is superior, or that it reduces cardiovascular events. For people on insulin or sulfonylureas, the trial evidence required medication reduction [24]. People with established cardiovascular disease, cancer or frailty are the groups where observational concerns are most pronounced, though those associations may reflect illness [59][62]. Mukamal's guidance (do not abandon TRE if it helps maintain weight, but food quality matters more) is a reasonable summary of expert caution [62].

These are projections, not findings. NY-TREAT completed in April 2026, so results may appear soon. As a doubly-labelled-water, 12-month trial, it could clarify mediation by energy intake [53]. Larger trials would need energy-matched comparators, at least 12 months of follow-up, objective adherence and intake measures, and adequate power to test non-inferiority. A hard-endpoint trial is the key inflection, but the 2026 ACC statement's framing suggests recommendations will keep resting on totality of evidence [50].
