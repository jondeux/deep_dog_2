## Final Report

# Time-Restricted Eating and Intermittent Fasting versus Continuous Calorie Restriction: How Strong Is the Evidence for Long-Term Cardiometabolic Benefit in Adults?

*Evidence reviewed to 10 November 2026. The question assessed: does time-restricted eating (TRE) or intermittent fasting (IF) improve long-term body weight, blood pressure, glycaemic control and cardiovascular events more than continuous calorie restriction (CCR) in adults? Certainty ratings labelled "independent judgement" are this review's own appraisal, not published GRADE ratings.*

## Executive Summary

**Bottom line:** the best available evidence shows TRE and IF are broadly equivalent to CCR for short- and medium-term weight loss and for most cardiometabolic markers. It does not show superiority. There is no randomised evidence on cardiovascular events, and almost no evidence beyond 12 months.

- **Body weight.** A 2025 BMJ network meta-analysis (99 randomised trials, 6,582 adults) found alternate-day fasting (ADF) lost 1.29 kg more than CCR (95% CI −1.99 to −0.59, moderate certainty). That falls below the authors' 2 kg threshold for clinical importance [1][2]. The Cochrane review found IF "may be as effective as" CCR, with changes "clinically insignificant" at short-term follow-up [3]. The 12-month TREATY trial found no significant advantage for adding an 8-hour window to a prescribed calorie deficit [4][5].
- **Blood pressure.** Direct IF-versus-CCR pooling shows no clear difference (SBP +0.93 mmHg, 95% CI −2.74 to 4.61) [6]. Small BP reductions appear mainly against unrestricted eating [7]. Independent judgement: low certainty.
- **Glycaemic control.** Fasting glucose does not differ from CCR [3][6]. HbA1c effects of TRE against unrestricted eating are tiny (−0.08%) [8]. Independent judgement: low certainty of no meaningful difference.
- **Cardiovascular events.** No randomised trial reporting CV mortality or major adverse cardiovascular events was found [3]. A peer-reviewed observational analysis links eating windows under 8 hours to higher CV mortality (hazard ratio [HR] 2.35), but confounding is unresolved [9].

**Main limitations:** short trials (mean 12 weeks in the BMJ network meta-analysis), surrogate outcomes, heterogeneous comparators, and calorie intake that is rarely matched or measured. Several named trials in the original outline (Carter, Schübel, Harvie, Jamshed, fasting-mimicking diets) and any 2024–2026 long-duration trials could not be verified against primary sources and are not characterised here.

## Framing: The Comparator Determines the Conclusion

The evidence splits into three comparisons that answer different questions. The first is regimen versus unrestricted eating or usual care, which shows whether any dietary intervention works. The second is regimen versus CCR, the active comparator that this question concerns. The third is a regimen plus calorie restriction versus calorie restriction alone, which isolates the contribution of timing.

Most TRE meta-analyses use the first comparison. The Frontiers 2025 meta-analysis (11 trials, 653 participants) excluded calorie restriction in either arm and compared TRE only with unrestricted feeding [7]. The JAMA Network Open 2024 meta-analysis (29 trials) used mostly "usual diet or no time restriction" controls [8]. A positive finding there therefore says little about superiority to CCR.

Definitions matter too. IF covers alternate-day fasting (about 500 kcal on fast days with free eating otherwise [10]), whole-day fasting and 5:2-type schedules. TRE covers windows of roughly 6–10 hours [11]. "Long-term" is taken here as 12 months or more. Very few comparisons reach it.

## Key Randomised Controlled Trials

### TRE added to prescribed calorie restriction: the TREATY trial

The cleanest test of timing against energy deficit is the TREATY trial (Liu et al., NEJM 2022). It was a single-centre, observer-blind trial in Guangzhou with 139 adults (BMI 28–45) randomised to 8-hour TRE (8:00 am–4:00 pm; n=69) or daily calorie restriction (n=70) for 12 months [5]. Both arms had identical calorie targets: 1,200–1,500 kcal/day for women and 1,500–1,800 kcal/day for men [5].

Weight fell 8.0 kg (95% CI −9.6 to −6.4) with TRE and 6.3 kg (−7.8 to −4.7) with CCR. The net difference of −1.8 kg (−4.0 to 0.4; P=.11) was not statistically significant [4][5]. Net differences were also non-significant for body fat (−1.5 kg, −3.1 to 0.2), lean mass (−0.3, −1.1 to 0.5), glucose, insulin, lipids and blood pressure [5].

One secondary summary describes the −1.8 kg difference as "favoring calorie restriction alone". The reported means show the opposite numerically: the TRE arm lost more, but the interval spans zero [5]. The authors' conclusion is that calorie restriction "explained most of the favorable effects", not the eating period [5].

Reported completion was about 85% and adherence above 80%, with no serious adverse events [4]. The NEJM paper itself could not be accessed. Every TREATY figure comes from two secondary summaries that agree with each other, and HbA1c is not reported in either.

### TRE versus unrestricted or habitual eating

The TREAT trial (Lowe et al., JAMA Internal Medicine 2020) is the verified primary source here. It randomised 141 adults with overweight or obesity (116 analysed) to 16:8 TRE (eating 12:00–8:00 pm) or consistent three-meal timing for 12 weeks [12]. Calories were not prescribed or measured, so it is a timing-only contrast [12].

- **Weight:** the between-group difference was −0.26 kg (−1.30 to 0.78; P=.63) in the total cohort and −1.13 kg (−2.33 to 0.07; P=.07) in the in-person subcohort [12].
- **Blood pressure:** SBP differed by 2.17 mmHg (−3.18 to 7.52) and DBP by −1.08 (−6.67 to 4.52) between groups [12].
- **Glycaemia:** no significant glycaemic or insulin differences were reported [12].
- **Lean mass:** lean mass fell 1.10 kg (−1.73 to −0.48) within the TRE arm. The between-group total lean mass difference was not significant (−0.75 kg, −1.96 to 0.45), but the appendicular lean mass index difference was (−0.16 kg/m², −0.27 to −0.05; P=.005) [12].
- **Limitations:** the authors state there was no self-reported intake, protein was not measured, and DXA did not account for hydration. Control-arm weight loss indicated that merely taking part in a weight-loss study produced short-term loss [12].

Two smaller trials are known only through a secondary commentary [4]. Cienfuegos 2020 (n=20, 8 weeks) found a spontaneous 550 kcal/day reduction and about 3% weight loss. Sutton 2018, in men with prediabetes, reported improved insulin sensitivity and blood pressure "even when body weight is maintained constant". That claim is from a very small, short study with no effect sizes available here, so it is hypothesis-generating only.

### Intermittent fasting versus daily calorie restriction

The Trepanowski 2017 trial (JAMA Internal Medicine, PMID 28459931) is the principal long IF-versus-CCR comparison. It followed about 100 adults with obesity for one year [10][13]. ADF (about 500 kcal on fast days, free eating otherwise) was compared with daily restriction of about 25% (about 1,500 kcal/day) [10]. Weight loss was 6.0% with ADF and 5.3% with daily restriction, with no significant difference. Both beat the control group [10].

Confidence intervals, attrition, adherence and BP, glucose and lean-mass results were not available in the sources reached. The weight figures come from a press summary issued by the principal investigator's own institution, so they are not independent [10].

Attrition in IF trials can be severe. A 12-month study in low-income women with obesity reported a 53.44% dropout in IF groups, which the commentary says may reduce power and introduce selection bias [4].

### Trials not verified

The original outline named Carter 2018 and Schübel 2018 (5:2 versus continuous restriction), Harvie 2011, Jamshed 2022 (early TRE) and fasting-mimicking diet trials. No primary or reliable secondary source for any of them could be read. No 2024–2026 trial of 12 months or longer comparing TRE or IF with CCR was identified, so none is claimed to change the conclusions.

## Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses

### BMJ 2025 network meta-analysis (Semnani-Azad et al.)

This is the most recent and largest review identified. It pooled 99 randomised trials in 6,582 adults (average age 45, 66% female, about 90% with existing health conditions). Searches ran to 14 November 2024 and durations were 3–52 weeks (mean 12) [1][2]. Certainty was assessed with GRADE.

ADF versus CCR gave −1.29 kg (−1.99 to −0.59; moderate certainty), ADF versus TRE −1.69 kg (−2.49 to −0.88) and ADF versus whole-day fasting −1.05 kg (−1.90 to −0.19). All three are below the 2 kg minimal clinically important threshold the authors set for obesity [1][2].

All IF strategies and CCR reduced weight modestly against unrestricted diets. Trials of 24 weeks or longer (n=17) showed benefit only against unrestricted eating, not against CCR [1][2]. There were no differences for HbA1c or HDL cholesterol [2]. ADF lowered total cholesterol, triglycerides and non-HDL cholesterol relative to TRE, and TRE raised total and LDL cholesterol slightly relative to whole-day fasting [2].

The authors conclude that IF has "similar benefits to continuous energy restriction" and that longer trials are needed [1]. Clinician commenters noted that a 1 kg difference is not clinically meaningful and that adherence limits feasibility [2]. Both accessible sources are secondary summaries because the BMJ page returned an access error. Pooled BP, fasting glucose, HOMA-IR (the insulin-resistance index), heterogeneity and risk-of-bias figures were not available.

### Cochrane review 2021 (Allaf et al.)

The draft framed this as a weight-loss review. It is in fact a review of IF for the prevention of cardiovascular disease, with weight as a secondary outcome (CD013496; search 12 December 2019; 26 studies, 18 pooled, 1,125 participants; durations 4 weeks to 6 months) [3]. No study reported mortality, cardiovascular mortality, stroke, myocardial infarction or heart failure [3].

### Other pooled analyses

- **Xu et al. 2023 (Frontiers in Nutrition).** This is the only direct intermittent-versus-continuous restriction meta-analysis read: 16 articles, 20 trials, 1,511 adults with metabolic-syndrome risk factors, searched to May 2022 [6]. Weight differed by −0.8 kg (−1.26 to −0.33; I² 0%). SBP was 0.93 mmHg (−2.74 to 4.61; I² 74%), DBP 1.15 (−0.24 to 2.55) and fasting glucose −0.02 mmol/L (−0.10 to 0.05) [6]. By duration, the weight difference was −0.75 kg at ≤4 weeks, −1.12 kg at >4–12 weeks and −0.53 kg (−1.35 to 0.29) beyond 12 weeks. The advantage disappears in the longest trials [6]. HbA1c, HOMA-IR and GRADE were not reported, and the sign convention was not stated in the extract.
- **Liu et al. 2024 (JAMA Network Open).** This pooled 29 trials of TRE (2,485 participants; search to October 2023) against usual or no time restriction [8]. Weight fell 1.37 kg (−1.99 to −0.75; I² 73%; low certainty) and HbA1c fell 0.08% (−0.15 to −0.01; I² 85%; low certainty). Fasting glucose fell 1.15 mg/dL (−1.77 to −0.53). SBP changed by −0.54 mmHg (−2.42 to 1.33) and DBP by −1.14 (−2.41 to 0.14), both non-significant. Lean mass fell 0.42 kg (−0.70 to −0.15) and energy intake fell 164 kcal/day [8]. Twenty-two of 29 trials were at high risk of bias and none was at low risk [8]. The authors called the effects "small and of uncertain clinical importance" [8]. The printed LDL interval was internally inconsistent and fasting-glucose certainty differed between text (low) and table (moderate) [8].
- **Yi et al. 2025 (Frontiers in Nutrition).** This pooled 11 trials (653 non-diabetic adults) of TRE without calorie restriction against unrestricted feeding [7][11]. SBP fell 1.79 mmHg (−3.30 to −0.27), DBP 1.75 (−3.07 to −0.43), fasting glucose 2.65 mg/dL (−3.92 to −1.39) and HOMA-IR 0.58 (−0.81 to −0.35); heterogeneity was low (I² 0–14%). Lipids did not change [7]. Larger BP reductions appeared in participants with elevated baseline BP, but the subgroup difference was not significant (p=0.32) [7]. The Discussion's claim that TRE reduced HbA1c has no supporting result in the paper's Results [7]. There was no GRADE rating and no 12-month subgroup.

### Why the reviews differ

The apparent conflict between "TRE improves BP and glucose" [7] and "IF is no better than CCR" [3][6] is largely a comparator artefact. Reviews pooling against unrestricted eating capture the benefit of any calorie reduction. Reviews pooling against CCR capture only the incremental effect of the schedule. Additional sources of divergence are:

- **Trial duration thresholds:** Liu 2024 required at least 12 weeks, Yi 2025 at least 4 weeks, and the Cochrane review only 4 weeks to 6 months [3][7][8].
- **Energy restriction:** Yi 2025 excluded calorie restriction, and in Liu 2024 TRE users cut roughly 164 kcal/day [7][8].
- **Search dates:** the Cochrane search ended in 2019 and Xu's in 2022, so earlier reviews miss later trials [3][6].
- **Statistical heterogeneity:** I² reached 73% for weight and 85% for HbA1c in Liu 2024 [8], and 74% for SBP in Xu 2023 [6].

Trial overlap between reviews was not quantified in any source read, so the reviews should not be treated as independent confirmations. The BMJ network meta-analysis is the most recent and uses GRADE, but its sources do not report BP, glucose or heterogeneity.

## Evidence by Outcome

### Body weight

Against unrestricted eating, every strategy produces small losses, for example −2.88 kg for IF in the Cochrane review [3] and −1.37 kg for TRE [8]. Against CCR the effect shrinks to roughly 1 kg or less. The estimates are −0.88 kg (CI to 0.00) [3], −0.8 kg [6], −1.29 kg for ADF [2] and −1.8 kg (CI crossing zero) in TREATY [5]. All are below the 2 kg clinical threshold used in the BMJ network meta-analysis [1].

Beyond 12 weeks the incremental advantage fades. Xu found −0.53 kg (CI crossing zero) beyond 12 weeks [6], the Cochrane medium-term estimate crossed zero [3], and the BMJ network meta-analysis found benefit in trials of 24 weeks or longer only against unrestricted diets [1]. No weight-regain or maintenance data were found. Independent judgement: moderate certainty that any advantage over CCR is small and clinically marginal; low certainty beyond 12 months.

### Blood pressure

The strongest BP signal, about −1.8 mmHg systolic and diastolic, comes from TRE without calorie restriction against unrestricted eating [7]. Liu 2024 found no significant BP effect (SBP −0.54) [8]. Against CCR, Xu found no difference and high heterogeneity [6]. TREATY and TREAT found no significant BP differences [12][5]. No review compared clinic with ambulatory measurement. Independent judgement: low certainty, with no evidence of superiority to CCR.

### Glycaemic control

Fasting glucose against CCR is flat (−0.02 mmol/L) [3][6]. Against unrestricted eating, TRE lowers fasting glucose by 1.15–2.65 mg/dL [7][8] and HOMA-IR by 0.58 [7], and HbA1c by only 0.08% [8]. TREAT and TREATY found no significant glycaemic differences [12][5]. The BMJ network meta-analysis found no HbA1c difference across any comparison [2]. Independent judgement: low certainty that TRE or IF is equivalent to CCR for glycaemia. Type 2 diabetes-specific pooled HbA1c versus CCR could not be sourced.

### Lipids and body composition

TRE shows no meaningful lipid effect without calorie restriction [7]. In the BMJ network meta-analysis, ADF was more favourable than TRE for total cholesterol, triglycerides and non-HDL cholesterol [2]. For body composition, Liu 2024 found a 0.42 kg lean-mass loss with TRE (moderate certainty) [8]. Xu found fat-free mass loss of 0.49 kg (−0.92 to −0.05) with intermittent restriction, alongside 0.75 kg more fat loss [6]. In TREATY the net lean-mass difference was not significant [5]. Whether these losses matter for sarcopenia in older adults is not addressed by the sources.

### Cardiovascular events and mortality

The Cochrane review found no study reporting mortality, CV mortality, stroke, myocardial infarction or heart failure [3]. No later randomised trial with these endpoints was found. Searches covered the ADA Standards of Care and the ClinicalTrials.gov mirror reached; the official registry record returned no content.

The only registered long-duration trial identified is TIMET 2.0 (NCT07189234, University of California San Diego). It is a phase 2 trial in 140 adults with metabolic syndrome, comparing a consistent 10-hour eating window with nutritional counselling. It began 23 October 2025 and is due to finish in January 2029. Its registry mirror lists no outcomes [14]. At that sample size it is unlikely to be powered for cardiovascular events (inference), and its completion lies beyond 2027.

Inference from surrogates is weak. The measured effects are small (about 1 kg, 1–2 mmHg, 0.08% HbA1c) and drawn from short trials, so they cannot be extrapolated to event reduction.

## The Observational Warning Signal: Very Short Eating Windows and Cardiovascular Death

### What was reported

In March 2024 an AHA scientific-sessions abstract (Chen and Zhong, Shanghai Jiao Tong University) used US NHANES data from 2003–2018 and reported that eating windows under 8 hours were associated with a "91% higher risk" of cardiovascular death [15]. The AHA release states that abstracts are not peer-reviewed. The authors stressed that the finding does not mean TRE caused cardiovascular death [15]. A HealthDay report of the same abstract gave HR 1.96 for under 8 hours (12–16 hours as reference) in 20,078 adults, with 840 CV deaths. It reported HR 2.06 in adults with CVD and 1.64 for the 8–<10 hour window in adults with CVD [16]. Neither source gives confidence intervals, and the 91% and 1.96 figures do not match; this was not resolved [15][16].

The analysis was later published in peer-reviewed form: Chen, Xu, Van Horn, Manson, Tucker, Du, Feng, Rong and Zhong, *Diabetes & Metabolic Syndrome: Clinical Research & Reviews* (August 2025). It used 19,831 adults and a 12–14 hour reference window, with median follow-up of 8.1 years. The HR for CV mortality with eating under 8 hours was 2.35, with no robust association with all-cause or cancer mortality [9]. The result persisted across subgroups and 14 sensitivity analyses [9]. It is a different analytic sample from the abstract, which explains the different numbers. The 2025 paper's 95% CI could not be confirmed, and all figures here come from a news summary.

### Interpretations

**Cautionary reading.** The authors say that, despite residual confounding, people should be "extremely cautious" about adopting short eating windows for years. They note no human evidence supports it [9]. The journal's editor-in-chief called it a safety signal and advised against windows under 8 hours in people with heart disease, uncontrolled hypertension, frailty, eating disorders, pregnancy or diabetes. If TRE is used, he preferred a 10–12 hour earlier window [9].

**Sceptical reading.** Christopher Gardner (Stanford) noted that exposure was based on only two days of recalled intake and that nutrient density may offer an alternative explanation. He also asked whether the shortest-window group differed in weight, stress or cardiometabolic risk [15]. The authors acknowledge reliance on self-reported diet and unmeasured factors [15][9]. Reverse causation is plausible, since people who are unwell or food-insecure may eat in short windows, though no source tested it. The all-cause and cancer signals disappearing after adjustment [9] suggests the CV association may be fragile.

Observational association and randomised surrogate data cannot be reconciled directly. A randomised trial comparing eating windows with hard outcomes is what the editor called for [9]. No contradicting cohort study was located.

## Robustness and Certainty

- **Risk of bias:** 22 of 29 trials in Liu 2024 were at high risk and none was low [8]; 3 of 11 in Yi 2025 were high and 8 had some concerns, mainly due to non-adherence [7]. Participant blinding is impractical in dietary trials, and adherence to fasting days was self-reported and variable [6].
- **Heterogeneity:** I² reached 73–85% for weight and HbA1c in Liu 2024 [8] and 74% for SBP in Xu 2023 [6].
- **Duration:** BMJ trials ran 3–52 weeks with a mean of 12 [1]. Only 17 trials were 24 weeks or longer, and the Yi 2025 review had one 12-month trial [2][7]. The Cochrane maximum was 6 months [3].
- **Adherence and attrition:** TREATY reported about 85% completion [4]. TREAT had 25 of 141 randomised participants who never recorded weight [12], and one IF trial recorded 53% dropout [4].
- **GRADE:** BMJ rates the ADF-versus-CCR weight difference moderate [2], Cochrane very low to low [3], and Liu 2024 mostly low [8].
- **Calorie matching and mechanism:** this is the central interpretive question. TREATY, with identical calorie targets, found no timing effect [5]. In TREAT, modelled energy intake did not differ between groups, yet weight differences were small and non-significant [12]. Liu 2024 found TRE users reduced intake by about 164 kcal/day [8]. Yi 2025 notes that modest unintended weight loss means its trials were not isocaloric [7]. Together this supports energy reduction, not timing, as the main driver of weight outcomes. The circadian argument rests mostly on Sutton 2018's small, short early-TRE study in men with prediabetes [4], which has not been replicated here.

## Subgroup and Safety Evidence

Subgroup data are thin.

- **Overweight/obesity:** most trials enrol this population, including TREATY (BMI 28–45) and TREAT (BMI 27–43) [12][5]. About 90% of BMJ participants had existing health conditions [1].
- **Sex:** BMJ participants were 66% female [1]. In Liu 2024, calorie-distribution trials involved women only [8]. A bone-metabolism analysis of the ADF trial found no premenopausal versus postmenopausal differences, though the snippet is truncated [13].
- **Older adults and sarcopenia:** no age-specific data were found. The lean-mass losses above are the only available proxy [8][12].
- **Baseline blood pressure:** TRE lowered BP more in participants with elevated baseline, but this subgroup difference was not significant (p=0.32 for SBP) [7].
- **Type 2 diabetes:** TREATY excluded diabetes [5], and Yi 2025 included only non-diabetic adults [7]. Xu 2023 reported no T2D-specific subgroup [6]. Hypoglycaemia in people on insulin or sulfonylureas was not quantified in any source, though the journal editor lists diabetes among conditions in which short windows should be avoided [9].
- **Other populations:** the sources do not report shift workers, healthy-weight adults, or non-US and non-Chinese populations. TREATY was run in China [5] and the NHANES cohort is American [9].
- **Adverse events:** Cochrane reported mild headaches in some participants [3], and TREATY reported no serious adverse events [4]. Gallstones and eating-disorder risk were not found in any source read.

## Guidelines and Forward Look

The ADA Standards of Care 2026 (Section 3, prevention of diabetes) recommends "an evidence-based eating pattern (e.g., Mediterranean, low carbohydrate)". The first 100,000 of 159,129 characters read contained no mention of TRE or IF, but the final portion was not read [17]. A secondary summary of the 2025 Standards reportedly lists IF among beneficial patterns [17]. Guideline statements from the AHA, European Society of Cardiology and obesity societies were not found. Gardner's critique refers to the AHA's 2021 dietary guidance statement on popular dietary patterns [15].

Registries are the only forward-looking source. TIMET 2.0 (due January 2029) is the one identified registered long-term trial [14]. Projection (reasoning: sample size, phase, completion date): no randomised cardiovascular event data for TRE or IF is likely before the 2030s unless a large outcome trial is registered that these searches missed. The BMJ authors call for longer-duration trials [1], and the JAMA 2024 authors call for trials without prescribed energy restriction, larger samples and longer follow-up [8].

TRE and IF reliably beat unrestricted eating by small margins, but against an equivalent calorie deficit the margin vanishes or becomes clinically trivial. Proponents can point to an adherence or simplicity advantage and to small benefits in selected subgroups. Sceptics note that effects track energy reduction, trials are short and at high risk of bias, and the one hard-outcome signal points toward harm for very short windows. The weight of evidence currently favours the sceptical reading on efficacy against CCR. Neither side has long-term outcome data.

Key gaps to carry forward: primary-source verification of TREATY and Trepanowski details, the unverified earlier trials, any 2024–2026 long-duration RCTs, pooled HbA1c versus CCR in type 2 diabetes, guideline positions beyond the ADA, and a confirmed 95% CI for the 2025 NHANES analysis.
