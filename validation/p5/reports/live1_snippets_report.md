## Final Report

# Time-Restricted Eating and Intermittent Fasting Versus Continuous Calorie Restriction: How Strong Is the Evidence for Long-Term Cardiometabolic Health in Adults? (Evidence to November 2026)

## Executive Summary

There is no robust evidence that time-restricted eating (TRE) or intermittent fasting (IF) improves long-term cardiometabolic health more than continuous calorie restriction (CCR). Randomised trials that use CCR as the comparator mostly show broadly similar weight and glycaemic outcomes. The Liu NEJM 2022 and Lin 2023 TRE trials and the Trepanowski 2017 alternate-day fasting (ADF) trial found no added benefit. The Carter 2018 type 2 diabetes trial met a noninferiority criterion for intermittent restriction. One 12-month 4:3 IF trial reports modestly greater weight loss than daily restriction. The reported advantage coincides with unmatched energy intake and lower attrition, so it may reflect adherence rather than the fasting pattern.

No randomised trial retrieved reports myocardial infarction, stroke or mortality for TRE/IF. The Cochrane review concluded the evidence was insufficient to judge cardiovascular disease prevention [1]. The only hard-outcome signal is observational: a US survey (NHANES) analysis linking eating windows of 8 hours or less to higher cardiovascular mortality. Its dietary exposure is thin and it is heavily criticised, so it is best treated as hypothesis-generating.

## 1. Scope and Interpretation

The question concerns adults generally, with no restriction by region or protocol. "Long-term" is interpreted as follow-up of 12 months or more. The comparator matters: TRE/IF versus no intervention or ad libitum eating usually shows benefit, whereas TRE/IF versus CCR tests whether the pattern of restriction adds anything beyond the energy deficit. The Cochrane review compared IF with ad libitum feeding and with short- and medium-term CCR [1]. A 2024 JAMA Network Open meta-analysis of 29 trials compared TRE with standard care or nutritional advice, not CCR [2]. Several widely cited positive pooled results therefore do not answer the CCR question.

Terms used here: a surrogate endpoint is a biomarker (weight, blood pressure, HbA1c, lipids) standing in for clinical events; HOMA-IR is a fasting glucose and insulin index of insulin resistance; MACE is major adverse cardiovascular events. "Calorie-matched" refers to equal prescribed or measured energy intake across arms. Only controlled-feeding designs verify this well.

### 2.2 TRE versus CCR

Liu et al. (NEJM 2022) is the central TRE-versus-CCR trial. The reported 12-month change was -8.0 kg (95% CI -9.6 to -6.4) with time restriction plus calorie restriction versus -6.3 kg (95% CI -7.8 to -4.7) with calorie restriction alone [3]. The retrieved snippet gives an unstated difference of about 1.7 kg, and clinical-education coverage describes no added benefit from fasting on top of calorie counting [4]. The primary record was identified [19], but its full text, sample size and between-group interval were not retrieved. Because both arms were calorie-restricted, the trial tests the incremental value of a time window rather than a replacement for counting calories.

Lin et al. 2023 is often mislabelled. The draft brief called it a Nature Medicine trial, but it was published in Annals of Internal Medicine (doi 10.7326/M23-0052) [20][6]. It tested an 8-hour window (noon to 8 pm), not 16:8 as such, and the TRE arm did not count calories [6]. At 12 months, among 77 completers, weight loss versus control was about 4.6 kg for TRE and 5.4 kg for calorie restriction, a non-significant difference [5]. Two PubMed Central identifiers circulate for this trial (PMC10630127 and PMC11192144) and were not reconciled. Completer-only figures also overstate efficacy relative to an intention-to-treat analysis.

Pavlou et al. found TRE produced greater weight loss than control in adults with type 2 diabetes at 6 months, whereas the calorie-restriction arm's change (-1.78%) was not significant [7]. The ACP summary does not report adherence or energy matching. The implication that TRE outperformed calorie restriction is not supported by a between-arm test in the retrieved text. TREATY, in which TRE was added to calorie restriction, likewise found no superiority over daily restriction alone [17].

### 2.3 ADF and 5:2 versus CCR

In Trepanowski 2017, 100 adults with obesity were randomised to ADF, daily calorie restriction or control for one year [8]. Independent trade coverage agrees that ADF produced no greater weight loss than daily restriction [9], and no advantage for weight maintenance or cardioprotection, with higher dropout in the ADF arm [10]. A research summary of the mirror copy reported 6.0% (ADF) versus 5.3% (daily restriction) weight loss and 38% dropout in the ADF arm. The publisher's version was not checked, and no confidence intervals or blood pressure, lipid or glucose data were retrieved.

An Epocrates summary reports a 12-month between-group mean difference of 2.89 kg favouring fasting (95% CI 5.65 to 0.14, minus signs apparently dropped; P = 0.040) [21]. Its trial identity is unconfirmed. It conflicts with the usual reading of Trepanowski as null, so it should not be attributed to that trial. It may be the 4:3 trial discussed below, but the link was not confirmed.

For 5:2-type regimens, Carter et al. is the main retrieved trial: a 12-month noninferiority comparison in type 2 diabetes with HbA1c changes of -0.5% (continuous) and -0.3% (intermittent), a 0.2% difference that met equivalence [11]. The Norwegian and German 12-month 5:2 trials, and the Jamshed 2022 early-TRE trial, were not retrieved.

### 2.4 The positive trial: 4:3 intermittent fasting

A 12-month randomised trial of 4:3 IF versus daily restriction in adults with overweight or obesity (PubMed 40163873) reports modestly greater weight loss with fasting in the intention-to-treat analysis [13]. Both arms received the same behavioural support, which makes the comparison like-for-like in intervention intensity [14]. Reported attrition was lower with fasting (about 19% versus 30%) [22]. Coverage states energy intake was not matched between arms [15].

This is the strongest challenge to the "no advantage" reading. It also illustrates the adherence argument: the advantage may reflect easier adherence rather than a metabolic property of fasting. Journal, sample size and confidence interval were not retrieved, so the effect size cannot be reported with confidence.

### 2.5 Registered and ongoing trials

Searches of ClinicalTrials.gov and ISRCTN surfaced no trial verified to have cardiovascular event or 24-month endpoints. One lead, NCT04502329 (energy-restriction IF and metabolic syndrome biomarkers), was identified, but its design, dates and outcomes were not visible [23].

The BMJ network meta-analysis is the most relevant review for the CCR question. Its authors' headline is that IF is an effective alternative to traditional calorie restriction [29], echoed in the journal group's framing as "comparable" for weight loss [25]. Independent experts quoted by the Science Media Centre noted that longer trials are needed [30]. Contemporary coverage emphasises that both IF and CCR beat ad libitum eating [31][32], which means much of the apparent IF benefit in pooled data comes from the energy deficit shared with CCR.

Reviews diverge mainly because of comparator choice. The Cochrane review mixes ad libitum and CCR comparators [1]. The JAMA Network Open, BMC Nutrition and Frontiers meta-analyses use standard care or control diets [28][2][27]. The BMJ and BMC Medicine reviews use CCR [24][26]. Overlap between trials across these reviews is likely but was not quantified in the retrieved text. No meta-analysis restricted to trials of 12 months or longer was found.

## 4. Evidence by Outcome

### 4.1 Body weight and adiposity

On weight, the pattern in CCR-comparator trials is parity. Liu's between-arm gap of about 1.7 kg [3] was described as no added benefit [4]. Lin found TRE and calorie restriction non-significantly different [5]. Trepanowski found no ADF advantage [9]. The pooled exception is the BMJ network meta-analysis, where ADF's mean difference of about -1.29 kg versus CCR [25] is statistically notable but small. The 4:3 trial similarly reports a modest ITT advantage [13]. Whether about 1 to 3 kg over a year is clinically meaningful is a judgement call. It is small relative to the 6 to 8 kg losses seen in both arms of the larger trials [3].

For body composition, the 2025 meta-analysis of 13 trials (612 women with overweight or obesity) found no lean mass loss with TRE [33]. Lowe's early TRE arm lost more weight than controls but not more body fat [16]. Waist and fat-mass data against CCR were not retrieved.

### 4.2 Blood pressure

No CCR-specific pooled blood pressure estimate was retrieved. Evidence against control diets: Frontiers 2025 reports a diastolic reduction of 3.30 mmHg with IF and no significant systolic effect [27]. The Cochrane commentary summarises possible modest systolic improvement versus ad libitum eating [34]. The 18-month Nature Medicine trial reports blood pressure falling more in restriction arms than in standard care [12], which again signals a deficit effect rather than a fasting-specific one. Controlled-feeding evidence of blood pressure improvement without weight loss comes from Sutton [18], and Lowe reported greater diastolic improvement with early TRE [16]. A later isocaloric crossover found no cardiometabolic benefit (Section 5).

### 4.3 Glycaemic control

In type 2 diabetes, Carter's 12-month noninferiority result (HbA1c difference 0.2%) supports equivalence of intermittent and continuous restriction [11]. Pavlou's arm-level HbA1c figures appear only in search snippets and are not cited here [7]. In general adult populations, pooled IF versus control diets showed no significant fasting glucose or HbA1c effect [27][28]. Sutton's insulin sensitivity improvement in prediabetic men was under weight-stable feeding [18]. Hypoglycaemia and medication-adjustment data from diabetes trials were not retrieved.

### 4.4 Lipids and other markers

Lipids appear mainly as supporting context. ADF was superior to TRE for LDL-C in the BMC Nutrition meta-analysis of control-diet comparisons [28]. The Cochrane commentary notes possible modest improvement in total cholesterol versus ad libitum eating [34]. The draft's concern about LDL-C increases on some regimens was not verified in the retrieved material.

### 4.5 Hard cardiovascular events and mortality

The Cochrane review prespecified all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, stroke, myocardial infarction and heart failure as primary outcomes [1]. Its conclusion was that the evidence was insufficient to determine whether IF prevents cardiovascular disease [1]. No randomised trial reporting these events for TRE/IF versus CCR was found in the retrieved sources. Trial durations of 6 to 18 months, with samples of about 100 to a few hundred, cannot detect event differences. Surrogate improvements cannot be assumed to translate into event reduction, because trial-level weight and blood pressure changes in these studies reflect the shared energy deficit rather than a demonstrated causal pathway to events.

## 5. Calorie-Matched Versus Unmatched Comparisons

The evidence splits into three designs.

**CCR comparators with TRE added.** Liu and TREATY tested TRE plus restriction against restriction alone and found no superiority [4][17]. These are the cleanest evidence that an additional window adds little beyond calories.

**Unmatched intake by design.** In Lin, the TRE arm did not count calories [6], so equivalence with calorie restriction means TRE achieved a similar deficit without counting. In the 4:3 trial, unmatched energy intake and lower attrition accompanied a modest fasting advantage [15][22]. In both cases the comparison tests practical regimen performance, not mechanism.

**Controlled-feeding or isocaloric timing tests.** Here the evidence conflicts. Sutton et al. (Cell Metabolism 2018) fed prediabetic men enough to maintain weight and found early TRE improved insulin sensitivity, blood pressure and oxidative stress [18]. A randomised crossover in women with overweight or obesity, with near-constant calories, found that neither early nor late TRE improved insulin sensitivity or cardiometabolic and inflammatory markers [35]. The preprint reports that the internal clock shifted but cardiometabolic health did not improve [36]. The preprint is not peer-reviewed, and the news report [35] is secondary.

These two positions cannot both be right as general statements, but they differ in population (men with prediabetes versus women with overweight), likely window timing and design. Sutton's study is small and summary-level in the retrieved text, so neither result is conclusive. The larger point stands: where deficits are equal, advantages shrink, and any timing-specific effect is, at best, unproven. Self-reported intake is a further weakness in unmatched trials, since energy intake was not verified by controlled feeding in these CCR comparisons.

## 6. Robustness of the Evidence

**Risk of bias and blinding.** Dietary trials cannot blind participants. No risk-of-bias ratings were retrieved for the reviews, so formal judgements are not reported here. The Lin analysis of completers (n = 77) exemplifies how per-protocol analysis can flatter results [5].

**Heterogeneity and consistency.** I² and prediction intervals were not retrieved for any review. Qualitatively, trials differ in window (8-hour, early, 16:8), regimen (ADF, 5:2, 4:3), comparator and population. Review conclusions are consistent in direction (IF about equal to CCR for weight) but not in magnitude for ADF [25][26].

**Sample size and precision.** Typical trials are small: Trepanowski enrolled 100 people [8]. Lin's completers were 77 [5]. The CIs in Liu are wide, spanning roughly 3 kg [3]. A trial of this size cannot confidently exclude modest differences, which is why a "noninferiority" finding is not the same as proof of equivalence.

**Adherence and attrition.** Attrition differs by regimen, but not consistently in direction: higher dropout in ADF [10] versus lower attrition in 4:3 (about 19% vs 30%) [22]. This pattern implies that adherence is regimen-specific rather than a general advantage of fasting.

**Short versus long follow-up.** The longest trials are 12 to 18 months [11][12]. Meta-analyses of trials of 12 months or longer were not found. Weight regain after the intervention was not documented in retrieved sources.

**Certainty.** The BMC Medicine review's CINeMA ratings span "moderate-to-high" to "very-low-to-low" without a per-outcome breakdown [26]. No GRADE ratings for BMJ outcomes were retrieved. The Cochrane conclusion of insufficiency is the clearest certainty statement for hard outcomes [1].

## 7. Observational and Registry-Based Data

The NHANES (2003 to 2018) eating-window analysis began as a 2024 American Heart Association conference abstract (P192) attributed to Meng Chen and Victor Zhong [37]. It reported that eating within 8 hours was linked to a 91% higher risk of cardiovascular death [38]. Hazard ratio values varied across coverage: one trade report gives about 1.91 (95% CI 1.20 to 3.03) and notes the abstract itself says 1.96 [39]. Another outlet reports HR 2.35 (a "135% higher" risk) [40]. The 2.35 figure appears in only one retrieved source and is most likely an error or a different model, but this could not be confirmed.

A peer-reviewed paper later appeared (PubMed 40944446; PMC12610411). Its retrieved description reports a U-shaped association with the lowest mortality at about 11 to 12 hours and more than 50% higher cardiovascular mortality at 8 hours or less, strongest in older adults, men and White participants [41]. Record-level counts are 4,158 all-cause, 1,277 cardiovascular and 989 cancer deaths [42]. The paper's "over 50%" and the abstract's 91% suggest the estimate changed in publication, but the full-text hazard ratios were not retrieved. The subgroup pattern is a flag for heterogeneity, not a confirmed effect modifier [41]. Coverage differs on other outcomes. One outlet reports TRE did not appear to affect all-cause or cancer mortality [43], whereas the study record describes an association of ≤8 hours with higher all-cause mortality [42]. This conflict is unresolved.

Criticism is substantial. A letter signed by 34 experts argued that eating patterns were based on two days of data at one time point, that sleep and shift work were not accounted for, and that NHANES sampling weights were not used [44]. Cardiologist Dariush Mozaffarian questioned whether the short-window group captured people with busy schedules or other circumstances linked to worse health [45]. Further reporting cites small numbers in the under-8-hour group and unaccounted confounders such as smoking [43]. The AHA itself described the abstract as preliminary pending a peer-reviewed manuscript [46], and the authors state the association is not proof of causation [47][48]. The signal is therefore a hypothesis, not a demonstrated harm. It does, however, mean the absence of RCT hard-endpoint data cuts both ways: neither benefit nor harm is established. Other cohort studies on breakfast skipping and late eating were not retrieved.

## 8. Safety and Tolerability

A 2024 meta-analysis of 15 RCTs (1,365 participants with overweight or obesity) found IF was not associated with a greater risk of adverse events overall. A numerically higher dizziness risk appeared in the IF-alone subgroup for non-early TRE, not statistically significant (P = 0.08) [49]. A 2025 meta-analysis of 13 trials in women with overweight or obesity found no lean mass loss, and four studies reported only minor, self-resolving events such as hunger and headache [33]. Both bodies of evidence are short-term and restricted to people with overweight or obesity, and neither reports hard outcomes.

Not retrieved: hypoglycaemia rates and medication adjustment in diabetes trials, gallstones, bone effects, eating-disorder risk, pregnancy, and frailty. Cautions for insulin or sulfonylurea users are standard clinical concerns but were not sourced in the research round. Systematic harms reporting appears sparse, which is itself the finding supported here.

## 9. Subgroup Evidence

Type 2 diabetes is the best-represented subgroup, through Carter (12 months, equivalence on HbA1c) [11] and Pavlou (6 months, TRE greater weight loss than control) [7]. Each is a single trial, and arm-level detail is only partly verified. The lean-mass meta-analysis covers women with overweight or obesity [33], and Sutton enrolled prediabetic men [18]. Older adults, premenopausal versus postmenopausal women, ethnicity, shift workers and people with established cardiovascular disease have no retrieved randomised evidence. The only subgroup signal is observational: the NHANES association was described as strongest in older adults, men and White participants [41], which needs replication before it is treated as real.

## 10. Guidelines and Consensus Statements

The ADA Standards of Care 2026 (Section 5) reportedly covers IF and TRE as practical eating-management tools for people with diabetes without a formal recommendation. This is based on a search summary, and the exact wording is unverified [50]. A 2026 personal view argues the guideline should be updated, noting that despite more than 225 clinical trials, IF has not been recommended in the ADA Standards, and that the ADA has not reviewed the glycaemic effects of IF [51]. It is opinion, not guidance. The only AHA document retrieved is the 2017 scientific statement on meal timing and frequency, led by Krista Varady [52]. It is outdated for the present question. No ESC, NICE or WHO position and no 2025 to 2026 AHA scientific statement was found.

## 12. Synthesis and Interpretation

Most cardiometabolic change in these trials follows weight change, so equal deficits give similar results. That explains why TRE added to calorie restriction [4][17] and ADF versus daily restriction [9] look alike, and why pooled IF and CCR both beat ad libitum eating [31][32]. Aggregate equivalence can nevertheless hide individual differences: some people adhere to one regimen and fail at another. The 4:3 trial's lower attrition [22] and ADF's higher dropout [10] show that adherence differs by regimen without favouring fasting in general.

Proponents point to simplicity, adherence and circadian benefits [18][16]. Sceptics point to null results in matched designs [35][36], to the Cochrane insufficiency conclusion [1], and to the absence of long-term outcomes. The two positions are compatible once the question is split: fasting is a viable alternative way to reach a deficit, but there is no robust evidence that it provides a distinct benefit.

Projection (reasoned, not sourced): resolving the open questions would need pragmatic trials of 24 months or longer with objective intake verification, calorie-matched controlled-feeding timing trials in men and women, and large event-driven trials or registry-linked cohorts. None were identified as registered and nearing completion in this search.

## 13. Practical Implications

TRE or IF can reasonably be offered as an alternative to CCR for adults who prefer it, expecting weight loss and glycaemic effects comparable to CCR. In type 2 diabetes, intermittent restriction showed equivalent HbA1c at 12 months in one trial [11]. Claims of superiority, long-term cardiovascular protection or fasting-specific mechanisms are not supported. Conversely, the observational mortality signal does not justify claims of harm, but it argues for caution about very short windows until better data exist [46][44]. Clinical use in people on glucose-lowering medication, older adults and people with eating-disorder history rests on caution rather than trial-based reassurance.

**Bottom line:** Evidence of parity with CCR for weight and glycaemia is moderate-to-low certainty. Evidence of superiority is weak. Evidence on cardiovascular events is absent.
