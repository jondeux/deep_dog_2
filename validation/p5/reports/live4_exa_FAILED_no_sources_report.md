## Scope and Interpretation of "Long-Term"

The brief covers adults of any subgroup, TRE window, IF protocol, region or publication date. For surrogate outcomes (weight, blood pressure, HbA1c), "long-term" is taken as 12 months or more of intervention or follow-up. For hard outcomes (cardiovascular events, mortality), it means multi-year follow-up. By this definition, most TRE trials are not long-term. Many last 4 to 12 weeks.

Definitions follow the brief. TRE restricts daily eating to a window, for example 16:8, 14:10 or 4–6 hours, with fixed or self-selected timing, early or late in the day. IF covers alternate-day fasting (ADF, including modified ADF) and 5:2 patterns. CCR is a daily energy deficit, typically 15–40% below estimated needs. Comparators include unrestricted eating, usual diet and "consistent meal timing" controls. How each trial prescribed calories determines how any comparison should be interpreted.

## Randomised Trials: TRE

### TRE vs CCR

The central head-to-head TRE trial is Liu et al. (NEJM, 2022, so older than two years) [16][10][11]. From background knowledge, this Chinese trial randomised roughly 139 adults with obesity to an 8-hour eating window plus calorie restriction, or to calorie restriction alone, for 12 months. Both arms were given the same prescribed energy target. Weight loss was substantial in both arms, and the difference was not statistically significant (about 1.8 kg, with a confidence interval that included zero). Waist circumference, fat mass, blood pressure and glycaemic measures also did not differ meaningfully between arms. This is a direct test of timing independent of energy deficit, and it is the strongest single piece of evidence supporting the "timing adds little" hypothesis. The caveats are that it was a single-country study and the TRE adherence data were self-reported.

Other TRE vs CCR trials exist (for instance Pavlou et al. 2023 in veterans, and multi-arm trials such as Lin et al. 2023 in Cell Metabolism and Teong et al. 2023). Background recall suggests that results align with Liu, with no clear TRE advantage once calories were matched. Their results are not verified here, and none of their pages was located. The brief asked specifically for 2025–2026 head-to-head trials. A Europe PMC query for this was constructed [7], but its results were not readable, so no 2025–2026 trial can be reported.

### TRE vs Unrestricted Eating or Usual Diet

TREAT (Lowe et al., JAMA Internal Medicine 2020, older than two years) compared 16:8 TRE with consistent meal timing for 12 weeks [13]. In recalled terms, weight change did not differ significantly between groups, and appendicular lean mass fell in the TRE arm, raising a lean-mass concern. This matters for interpretation. It suggests that without prescribed calorie targets, TRE may produce only small weight changes in practice, because a shorter window does not reliably create a deficit.

Early TRE (eTRE) studies, such as Sutton et al. (2018, older than two years, small), reported improved insulin sensitivity and blood pressure in men with prediabetes over five weeks, apparently independent of weight loss. Because that was a small crossover study with a short duration, it is hypothesis-generating. Studies of 4–6 hour windows (for example, Cienfuegos et al., 2020) reported modest weight loss over about eight weeks versus controls. Single-arm trials in metabolic syndrome (for example, Wilkinson et al., 2020) suggested improvements but lacked a control group.

### Early vs Late Windows

The circadian rationale is that eating earlier aligns with peaks in insulin sensitivity and thermogenesis. The clinical evidence is thinner than the mechanistic rationale. Direct early-versus-late randomised trials are few, short and small, and I could not retrieve a list of them. This remains an open gap.

## Randomised Trials: IF

### IF vs CCR

Trepanowski et al. (JAMA Internal Medicine, 2017, now older than two years) is the main 12-month ADF vs daily CR trial [12][17]. As recalled, about 100 metabolically healthy adults with obesity were randomised to ADF, daily CR (25% below needs) or control. Weight loss at 12 months was similar in ADF and CR (roughly 6% vs 5%), and neither differed significantly in blood pressure or glycaemic measures. Dropout was highest in the ADF arm. LDL cholesterol rose in the ADF group, so ADF showed no cardioprotective advantage. This trial is an important counter to the idea that IF is simpler to adhere to.

In type 2 diabetes, 5:2 vs continuous restriction trials (such as Carter et al., 2018, Australia) reported similar HbA1c and weight outcomes over 12 months, with no clear IF advantage. Earlier trials by Harvie and colleagues similarly found IF comparable, not superior, to CCR on weight and insulin resistance.

Concerns include hypoglycaemia in people on insulin or sulfonylureas, lean-mass loss, gallstones, fatigue and eating-disorder risk. I could not verify any of these against retrieved text. Reviews that pool IF or TRE against unrestricted diet tend to show weight loss, driven largely by unintended calorie reduction. Reviews restricted to head-to-head comparisons with CCR tend to show little or no difference. Differences in inclusion criteria, minimum duration, comparator definition, and fixed-effect vs random-effects models also affect conclusions.

Two positions can be stated, both unverified here. Proponents argue that TRE/IF is an effective, simpler alternative to CCR. Critics argue that effects are small, mostly attributable to calorie reduction, and rest on low-certainty evidence. On methodological grounds, the critics' reading is better supported when the question is superiority over CCR, because the most rigorous direct comparisons (Liu 2022, Trepanowski 2017) found no advantage. The proponents' reading holds when the question is non-inferiority or acceptability.

## Outcome-by-Outcome Synthesis

**Body weight and composition.** Across recalled head-to-head trials, between-group differences are small and mostly not statistically significant. Whether any difference exceeds the ≥5% weight-loss threshold for clinical meaning depends on the arms. In both Liu and Trepanowski, both arms exceeded it. Lean-mass loss appears in some TRE trials (TREAT) and is not reliably assessed in others.

**Blood pressure.** I could not verify pooled effects. Trial-level recall suggests small reductions that track weight loss and do not differ between TRE/IF and CCR. eTRE findings of blood pressure benefit independent of weight loss come from small, short studies.

**Glycaemic control.** In type 2 diabetes, 5:2 vs continuous restriction showed similar HbA1c. Evidence for independent effects of timing on insulin sensitivity is limited to short, small trials.

**Cardiovascular events and mortality.** To the author's knowledge, no randomised trial of TRE or IF vs CCR is powered for, or reports, cardiovascular events or mortality. A search for events and mortality in RCTs could not be completed, so this is a negative claim from background knowledge, not a verified finding.

**Other markers.** The LDL rise in ADF in Trepanowski is the one recalled lipid signal. Liver fat and inflammation data could not be retrieved.

### Criticisms and How to Weigh the Signal

The generic criticisms are well known and methodological. Eating windows were defined from two days of self-reported dietary recall and then extrapolated to long-term habits. Residual confounding, reverse causation (illness may shorten eating windows) and healthy-user effects are likely. Diet quality, shift work and baseline illness were incompletely captured, subgroup event counts were low, and no causal inference is possible. The researchers' own position, as recalled, was that the finding was associational and should prompt caution and further research, not a change in guidance. Named expert critics and their affiliations were not retrieved; a Science Media Centre search page was located but not read [30].

An observational association of this type should be treated as hypothesis-generating. It should not outweigh randomised evidence on surrogate outcomes, nor be dismissed as evidence of harm either. The two evidence types answer different questions. RCTs show short-term effects on weight, blood pressure and glucose. The cohort signal concerns rare hard events in free-living populations. The resolving design would be a long-term randomised trial with event outcomes, or a prospective cohort with repeated, validated measurement of eating windows.

## Robustness of the Evidence

Risk of bias is structurally elevated because participants cannot be blinded to timing or fasting, so outcome assessors need blinding and objective measures. Sample sizes are typically in the low hundreds or fewer. Liu (about 139) and Trepanowski (about 100) were adequately powered only for weight differences of around 2–3 kg or more, which is a limitation for detecting smaller timing effects.

Adherence and attrition differ across regimens. In Trepanowski, dropout was highest with ADF, which undercuts the claim that fasting is easier to sustain. Intention-to-treat versus completer handling needs checking in each paper.

Calorie matching is the pivotal issue. In Liu, both arms had the same prescribed calories, so the null result isolates timing. In TRE-only trials without calorie targets, any weight loss may reflect incidental deficit. Intake relies on self-report, which has well-known under-reporting error. Surrogate endpoints (weight, blood pressure, HbA1c) predict events imperfectly, and the extent to which they do in this setting is untested. GRADE ratings, funnel-plot or Egger results, registry-versus-publication discrepancies and conflicts of interest (for instance, author ties to diet books or products) could not be verified.

## Interpretation: Why Results Look the Way They Do

The simplest mechanism is energy deficit. Shortening the eating window or fasting on set days tends to reduce intake, and weight follows intake. When trials equalise prescribed calories, as in Liu, the extra effect of timing largely disappears. A plausible second mechanism is circadian alignment and insulin dynamics, which could produce weight-independent metabolic benefit. The only supportive evidence recalled is small and short (eTRE studies), so this remains unproven at the clinical level.

The adherence argument for TRE/IF has mixed support. Trepanowski showed higher dropout with ADF, and TRE adherence is often self-reported. Effects appear to fade after about six months in several weight-loss trials, though this pattern could not be verified here. Individual variation and responder subgroups are plausible but not established.

## Ongoing Research and Outlook

No registry entry (ClinicalTrials.gov, ISRCTN, ChiCTR) was retrieved, so I cannot list ongoing long-term or event-outcome trials. As a projection, not a finding, a large event-outcome trial of TRE is unlikely to report before the early 2030s, given the cost and follow-up needed. Evidence that would change the conclusion includes a well-powered 12–24 month calorie-matched trial showing a consistent timing advantage, or a peer-reviewed replication of the cardiovascular-mortality signal with better exposure measurement.

## Conclusion and Practical Implications

The best-supported statement is this. For weight, blood pressure and glycaemic control, TRE and IF look roughly equivalent to CCR when energy deficit is comparable, with low-to-moderate certainty. That judgement rests on recalled trial results, and no GRADE rating was verified. For cardiovascular events and mortality, the evidence is essentially absent from randomised trials. The one prominent signal is a conference-abstract-level observational association that cannot establish harm or benefit.

In practice, clinicians can reasonably say that TRE or IF is an acceptable option for people who find it easier to follow than daily restriction, but that it is not shown to be superior. Extra caution is warranted for people on glucose-lowering drugs, older adults at risk of lean-mass loss, and anyone with eating-disorder history. The most important next studies are long (12 months or more), calorie-matched, adequately powered head-to-head trials with objective intake measurement and systematic safety reporting. Before this report is relied on for specific figures, each trial and review named above should be checked against its primary publication.
