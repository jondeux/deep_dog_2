## Final Report

# Time-Restricted Eating and Intermittent Fasting versus Continuous Calorie Restriction: How Strong Is the Evidence for Long-Term Cardiometabolic Health in Adults? (Evidence to 10 November 2026)

## Executive Summary

**Core question.** Does time-restricted eating (TRE) or intermittent fasting (IF: alternate-day fasting, the 5:2 pattern, other periodic fasting) improve long-term weight, blood pressure, glycaemic control and cardiovascular events more than continuous calorie restriction (CCR) in adults?

**Direct answer.** The evidence does not show an advantage over CCR, and its strength for "long-term" outcomes is low.

- **Weight.** Head-to-head trials and the 2026 Cochrane review point to broad equivalence with conventional dieting. The Cochrane review (22 randomised controlled trials, 1,995 adults, follow-up up to 12 months) found "little to no difference" in weight versus traditional dietary advice, and "likely little to no difference" at moderate certainty versus no intervention [1][2].
- **Blood pressure and glycaemia.** Evidence is sparse and mixed. TRE without calorie restriction modestly lowered blood pressure against unrestricted eating in one 2025 meta-analysis [3]. An umbrella review reported IF was *less* effective than CCR for systolic blood pressure [4]. The 12-month trial in type 2 diabetes found HbA1c changes comparable between 5:2 and CCR [5].
- **Cardiovascular events and mortality.** No randomised trial reporting hard endpoints was identified. The only mortality signal is observational and unfavourable to very short eating windows. It comes from a non-peer-reviewed American Heart Association (AHA) abstract, and its credibility is limited [6][7].
- **Where evidence is weakest.** Duration (most trials are 12 weeks to 12 months), hard endpoints, calorie-matched designs that isolate timing, and subgroups (older adults, postmenopausal women, existing cardiovascular disease).

The strongest honest conclusion is that TRE and IF are *an option* roughly equivalent to CCR for weight, with uncertain benefits for other outcomes and unknown effects on events.

## Scope, Interpretation of "Long-Term" and Source Quality

"Long-term" is interpreted as 12 months or more for surrogate outcomes and any follow-up for hard endpoints. Most trials fall short of this. The Cochrane review's own included trials ran up to 12 months, and its authors state that longer follow-up is needed [1][2]. The Cochrane search ended on 5 November 2024, and the most recent TRE meta-analysis located searched to April 2025 [3]. Trials published after those dates were not captured by any retrieved synthesis, and no 2025–2026 randomised trial of 6 months or more was identified in this research.

Source quality varies and is flagged inline throughout:

- Primary journal pages for several landmark trials (the New England Journal of Medicine, JAMA Internal Medicine, PubMed, the Cochrane full text) were not readable, because of access blocks.
- Some trial figures therefore come from secondary appraisals, repository abstracts or search snippets. Each is labelled as such.
- No effect size is stated here without a source that contains it.

### TRE versus calorie restriction

Liu and colleagues randomised 139 adults with obesity to TRE plus calorie restriction or calorie restriction alone [9]. Both arms received identical calorie prescriptions (1,500–1,800 kcal/day for men, 1,200–1,500 for women) and macronutrient targets [8]. Weight fell by 8.0 kg (95% CI −9.6 to −6.4) with TRE plus restriction and 6.3 kg (−7.8 to −4.7) with restriction alone. The net difference was −1.8 kg (p=0.11) [8]. The printed confidence interval in the appraisal appears to contain a typographical error, probably −4.0 to 0.4 [8].

Two points temper both optimistic and pessimistic readings. First, the appraisal (a secondary source, not the NEJM page) reports that eating windows were nearly identical across arms, about 10 h 20 min per day by the reviewer's reading. The NEJM supplement apparently reports about 8 h vs 11 h, and the reviewer flagged uncertainty over which figures are correct. If the windows were nearly identical, the null result says little about timing itself [8]. Second, physical activity was uncontrolled and recruitment was advertisement-based [8]. The trial excluded diabetes and recent cardiovascular disease [8]. The 84.9% retention at 12 months is good, and adherence was above 80% in both arms [8].

Tricò and colleagues tested early time-restricted carbohydrate intake against a Mediterranean-style diet in 27 older adults with type 2 diabetes, with both arms energy-restricted to a 5% weight-loss target [10]. After 12 weeks, HbA1c fell 3 mmol/mol (about 0.2 percentage points) with early carbohydrate restriction and 4 mmol/mol (about 0.3 points) with Mediterranean dieting (between-group p=0.386) [10]. The authors concluded there were "no additional metabolic benefits compared with conventional dieting" [10]. The trial was powered only to detect a 4 mmol/mol difference, used self-reported food diaries, and enrolled a 100% white population [10].

### TRE versus usual eating

TREAT randomised 116 adults to a 16:8 eating window or consistent meal timing for 12 weeks, with no calorie prescription [12]. Weight change was −0.94 kg (95% CI −1.68 to −0.20) with TRE and −0.68 kg (−1.41 to 0.05) with consistent timing, which was not a significant difference [12]. Lipids, glucose and insulin did not differ [12]. Appendicular lean mass index differed by −0.16 kg/m² against TRE [11]. The authors flagged this as "raising caution" [12]. The 12-week duration cannot speak to 12-month outcomes [11]. Arm-level attrition and blood pressure values were not available in retrieved sources.

### Alternate-day fasting versus CCR

Trepanowski and colleagues ran the best-known long ADF trial. It enrolled 100 metabolically healthy adults with obesity (86 women, mean BMI 34), and randomised them to ADF (25% of energy needs on fast days, 125% on feast days), daily CR (75% of needs) or control for 12 months [13].

- **Weight.** At 12 months, weight loss against control was −6.0% (95% CI −8.5 to −3.6) for ADF and −5.3% (−7.6 to −3.0) for CR, with no significant difference [13].
- **Attrition.** Dropout was highest with ADF (38%), against 29% for CR and 26% for control [13].
- **Cardiometabolic markers.** Blood pressure, glucose, insulin and C-reactive protein did not differ between ADF and CR. LDL cholesterol was 11.5 mg/dL higher with ADF at 12 months (95% CI 1.9 to 21.1) [13].
- **Adherence.** ADF participants ate above the prescription on fast days and below it on feast days. The authors said many "converted their diet into de facto calorie restriction" and that ADF "may be less sustainable in the long term" [13][14].

The trial was NIH-funded [14]. The editors revised Healio's headline from "ineffective" to "not superior" [14]. That is the correct reading: ADF worked about as well as CR and was not worse for weight.

### 5:2 and periodic fasting versus CCR

Carter and colleagues randomised 137 adults with type 2 diabetes (mean HbA1c 7.3%, BMI 36.0) to 5:2 intermittent restriction (500–600 kcal on two non-consecutive days) or continuous restriction (1,200–1,500 kcal/day) for 12 months [5].

- **HbA1c.** The change was −0.5% (standard error 0.2) with continuous and −0.3% (0.1) with intermittent restriction. The difference was 0.2% (90% CI −0.2 to 0.5), within the ±0.5% equivalence margin [5].
- **Weight.** Intermittent restriction lost 6.8 kg and continuous lost 5.0 kg (difference −1.8 kg, 90% CI −3.7 to 0.07). This did *not* meet the ±2.5 kg equivalence margin, so the study cannot claim equivalence for weight, and it was underpowered for it [5].
- **Safety.** Medications likely to cause hypoglycaemia were reduced at baseline. 16 of 46 participants on sulfonylureas or insulin were at risk, and early hypo- or hyperglycaemic events did not differ (3.2 vs 4.9, p=0.28) [5]. Press coverage warns that insulin and sulfonylurea users need monitoring and dose changes [16].
- **Completion.** Only 97 of 137 completed, and per-arm numbers were not stated [5].

Sundfør and colleagues' 12-month Norwegian trial in 112 adults with BMI 30–45 reportedly found −8.0 kg intermittent vs −9.0 kg continuous (p=0.6), with similar regain and risk-factor improvements and more hunger in the intermittent arm (p=0.002). These figures come from a search summary only and are unverified [15].

### Where the reviews agree and disagree

The reviews agree that TRE and IF beat *unrestricted eating* on weight by about 1–3 kg. They agree far less on whether they beat *calorie restriction*. The Cochrane review concluded that the approaches "did not differ in achieving weight loss, producing no clinically meaningful changes in most of the outcomes considered" [1]. The women's meta-analysis found no significant advantage when TRE was added to restriction [18]. Intermittent versus continuous restriction in healthy adults also shows no superiority [20].

The conflicting reading comes from Fernandes-Alves, whose isocaloric-control pool still shows −1.46 kg for TRE, and whose authors conclude TRE is favourable "even when intake is isocaloric" [17]. Three plausible reasons for the disagreement:

1. The abstract does not say how "isocaloric" was verified, and self-reported intake is error-prone [8].
2. The pooled comparators differ. They are not restricted to CCR but include diets matched for energy.
3. The effect is small, and its lower confidence bound (−0.26 kg) is close to zero.

The contradiction is not resolved by the retrieved evidence. A weight difference of about 1.5 kg against isocaloric controls would sit well below conventional ≥5% weight-loss thresholds.

## Findings by Outcome Domain

### Body weight and adiposity

Weight is the best-studied outcome and the one with the most consistent answer. Against CCR, the net difference at 12 months is small and non-significant: −1.8 kg in Liu (p=0.11) [8], and −6.0% vs −5.3% in Trepanowski [13]. Both arms in these trials exceeded 5% weight loss, so the clinically relevant question is whether TRE or IF adds anything, and the evidence says it does not reliably do so. Cochrane found weight versus traditional advice "little to no difference" [1]. The 5% weight-reduction outcome (4 studies, 472 participants) was uncertain [2].

Fat mass and lean mass matter as well. In Fernandes-Alves, fat-free mass fell 0.41–0.86 kg more with TRE than with controls [17]. TREAT recorded a lean-mass signal against TRE [11][12]. Whether this is clinically relevant is unresolved, and weight regain beyond 12 months is unreported in the retrieved sources.

### Blood pressure

The sources conflict, partly because the comparators differ.

- Against unrestricted eating, one meta-analysis found systolic and diastolic reductions of about 1.8 mmHg in non-diabetic adults [3]. The authors cautioned that weight loss may confound the effect [3].
- The women-only meta-analysis found no effect (SBP +0.38 mmHg, 95% CI −3.57 to 4.32) [18].
- Against CCR, the EClinicalMedicine umbrella review reported IF was *less* effective for systolic pressure [4].
- In the head-to-head trials, Trepanowski found no ADF-versus-CR difference in blood pressure [13], and Tricò found no change [10].

A drop of about 1.8 mmHg falls below the 2–5 mmHg range often cited as population-relevant. That range is a general convention, not drawn from the retrieved sources. No source shows blood-pressure benefit beyond that attributable to weight loss.

### Glycaemic control

In type 2 diabetes, 5:2 and CCR produced comparable HbA1c changes at 12 months, with the equivalence margin of ±0.5% met [5]. Tricò found comparable 12-week HbA1c changes between early time-restricted carbohydrate intake and Mediterranean dieting [10]. Both reductions (about 0.2–0.5 points) sit at or below the 0.3–0.5-point range often treated as clinically meaningful. That threshold is conventional, not from the retrieved sources.

In non-diabetic adults, TRE without restriction lowered fasting glucose by 2.65 mg/dL and HOMA-IR by 0.58 against unrestricted eating [3]. The women's meta-analysis found fasting insulin −2.12 µU/mL (95% CI −4.17 to −0.07) but no significant effect on glucose or HOMA-IR [18]. No pooled HbA1c estimate versus CCR was retrieved.

### Cardiovascular events and mortality

No randomised trial of TRE or IF reporting cardiovascular events or mortality was identified. A clinician-facing news summary notes that prior TRE research measured intermediate markers (blood pressure, glucose, cholesterol) rather than hard endpoints [7]. Cochrane's summary reports no event or mortality outcomes [1]. The only registered trial located, IMPACT-ADT (NCT07202247), is a Phase II study in prostate-cancer patients on androgen deprivation therapy, with the primary outcome of 10-year cardiovascular *risk score*, not events [21].

This is a negative finding from a limited search. Registry platforms (ClinicalTrials.gov, ISRCTN, ChiCTR) could not be systematically queried, so an unregistered or unretrieved event-endpoint trial cannot be excluded.

### Lipids and secondary outcomes

LDL cholesterol was higher with ADF than CR at 12 months (+11.5 mg/dL) in Trepanowski [13]. TRE without restriction had no meaningful lipid effect in one meta-analysis [3], and none appeared in the women's pool [18]. Quality of life showed little to no difference versus advice (3 studies, 106 participants) [1].

## Robustness of the Evidence

### Risk of bias, size and duration

Behavioural interventions cannot be blinded. Authors of the 2025 reviews cite this directly [18][3]. In the cardiometabolic meta-analysis, 8 of 11 trials had "some concerns" and 3 had high risk of bias [3]. Cochrane downgraded because "most included studies did not use the most robust methods, they included few people, and reported results that were inconclusive" [1]. Trial sizes are small: 27 in Tricò [10], 100 in Trepanowski [13], 116 in TREAT [12], 139 in Liu [9]. Pooled datasets are still modest, at 1,341 to 1,995 participants [1][17]. The Frontiers women's authors note small-study bias "cannot be excluded" despite an Egger test without significant asymmetry [18]. Pooled I² was 0% in the women's analysis, but moderate (50%) for BMI in the cardiometabolic one [18][3].

### Adherence and attrition

Attrition varies by protocol and by trial. Reported dropout or completion ranged as follows:

| Trial | Attrition |
|---|---|
| Liu (12 months) | 15% (84.9% completed) [8] |
| Tricò (12 weeks) | ~15% in both arms [10] |
| Trepanowski (12 months) | ADF 38%, CR 29%, control 26% [13] |
| Carter (12 months) | 97 of 137 completed overall (about 29% lost), per-arm figures not stated [5] |

Adherence was mostly self-reported. Tricò used 3-day food diaries, which the authors call "prone to error and bias" [10]. Liu relied on self-reported intake [8]. TREAT used a Bluetooth scale for weight only [12]. The Trepanowski deviations (overeating on fast days) show that "adherence" to fasting prescriptions is often partial [14].

### Calorie matching

Calorie matching decides whether a timing effect exists. Liu and Tricò prescribed equal energy in both arms and found no added benefit from timing [8][10]. TREAT did not prescribe energy [12]. Trepanowski prescribed energy by arm but ADF participants deviated [14]. Carter's arms had different regimens, and equal weekly deficit was not stated in the retrieved record [5]. A 2018 12-week TRE trial and a Cambridge British Journal of Nutrition trial, both cited secondarily, suggest TRE's benefit tracks calorie restriction. Neither was verified in this research [22]. The Frontiers cardiometabolic authors conclude weight loss is "a potential confounder when attributing cardiometabolic effects solely to meal timing" [3].

### Surrogate versus hard endpoints

Every randomised result above concerns surrogates (weight, blood pressure, HbA1c, lipids). Extrapolating from them to events is unvalidated for fasting regimens, and the LDL rise with ADF illustrates that surrogates can move in opposite directions [13]. The EClinicalMedicine umbrella review found only 10 of 103 significant associations were high certainty by GRADE [4].

### Certainty ratings

GRADE ratings were reported sparingly. Cochrane graded weight versus no intervention as moderate, and most other outcomes as low to very low, with certainty for weight versus traditional advice not stated on the retrieved page [1][2]. The 2025 TRE meta-analyses retrieved reported no GRADE ratings in the text read, even where methods mention it [18][3]. A full GRADE table by outcome and comparison could not be compiled from the sources.

## Controversies and Conflicting Findings

### Cardiovascular risk signal with short eating windows

An AHA scientific-session abstract (poster P192, updated 27 March 2024) analysed about 20,000 US adults from NHANES 2003–2018, linked to the National Death Index through December 2019. Median follow-up was 8 years, maximum 17 [6]. It reported a 91% higher risk of cardiovascular death with an eating window under 8 hours. It also reported a 66% higher risk of heart disease or stroke death with an 8-to-under-10-hour window among people with existing cardiovascular disease. TRE did not reduce all-cause mortality [6].

Its credibility is limited for several reasons:

- **No effect-size detail.** Hazard ratios, confidence intervals and death counts were not reported in the release [6].
- **Not peer reviewed.** The AHA says findings are "preliminary until published as a full manuscript" [6].
- **Exposure error.** Eating windows were classified from only two 24-hour recalls [6]. Christopher Gardner (Stanford) noted this and that nutrient density might explain the findings [6].
- **Calories and timing.** Calories were not calculated, and participants surveyed before 2012 were unlikely to be practising deliberate TRE [22].
- **Causation.** Senior author Victor Zhong stated the study "does not mean that time-restricted eating caused cardiovascular death" [6].

Gardner's comment also frames the two sides of the debate. The signal is consistent with short-term benefit and long-term harm, but nothing in the evidence distinguishes that from residual confounding or reverse causation [6]. Secondary coverage (HealthDay, LiveScience) repeats the AHA material and adds no independent evidence [7][23].

Two PubMed records suggest later peer-reviewed analyses, but neither could be read, and both are unverified leads:

- One reports a hazard ratio of 2.35 (95% CI 1.39–3.98) for cardiovascular mortality with eating under 8 hours versus 12–14 hours, with the authors noting residual confounding may explain the association [24].
- A second NHANES 2003–2018 analysis describes a U-shaped relationship with lowest risk around 11–12 hours and over 50% higher cardiovascular mortality at 8 hours or less [25].

If confirmed, these would mean the signal has appeared in at least one peer-reviewed analysis. They would still be observational analyses of the same NHANES source (or overlapping data), so they would not be independent replication. No contradicting 2024–2026 cohort analysis was retrieved.

### Timing effect versus calorie effect

Proponents of circadian-timing benefit point to Sutton and colleagues' early time-restricted feeding trial in men with prediabetes. It reportedly improved insulin sensitivity without weight loss in a short, controlled-feeding design, as described by Tricò and colleagues [10]. The primary Sutton page could not be retrieved, and its numerical claims are unverified [26]. Tricò also cite Andriessen and Che for glucose benefits of TRE in type 2 diabetes, but those did not match weight loss between arms. Teong found energy-restricted early TRE improved postprandial glucose only [10].

Critics counter that the longer, larger trials with calorie prescriptions (Liu, Tricò) show no added benefit [8][10]. The Liu trial itself is open to criticism for its near-identical eating windows [8]. Methodologically, the controlled-feeding trials answer a mechanistic question in small samples over short periods. The prescribed-calorie trials answer the pragmatic question that matters for clinical choice. The disagreement is partly about different questions rather than contradictory data.

### Equivalence versus superiority for IF

Head-to-head ADF and 5:2 trials show broadly similar results to CCR [13][5][15], and a meta-analysis in healthy adults agrees [20]. The Carter weight result (6.8 vs 5.0 kg, not meeting equivalence) shows how "comparable" can be asserted when the data are underpowered rather than demonstrably equal [5].

### Funding and spin

Funding was largely public: NIH for Trepanowski [14], a clinical nutrition society fellowship (ESPEN) and Italian diabetes society fellowships for Tricò [10], and no dedicated funding for the Cochrane review [1]. The retrieved sources contain no evidence of industry funding. Spin appears in headlines and abstracts more than in funding. Examples:

- The ADF trial was initially headlined "ineffective" [14].
- The 91% figure circulated without hazard ratios [6].
- Carter's authors call 5:2 "comparable" although the weight equivalence margin was not met [5].
- A press release described the same trial as "world-first" benefits, though the paper shows no between-group differences [16].

Safety considerations deserve explicit attention. In type 2 diabetes, hypoglycaemia risk requires medication adjustment [5][16]. Lean mass loss is a recurring signal [11][17]. Eating-disorder risk, pregnancy and bone-health data were not found in the retrieved sources. TRE variants (early versus late windows, window length) are essentially unaddressed by head-to-head randomised comparisons in the retrieved evidence. Liu tested a single window [8].

## Evidence Gaps and What Is Needed

The weakest points, ranked by consequence for the research question:

1. **No hard-endpoint randomised trials.** Neither event nor mortality data from trials were identified [7]. The sole identified registered trial uses a 10-year risk score [21].
2. **Little data beyond 12 months.** Cochrane's included trials ran up to 12 months and the authors call for longer follow-up [1].
3. **Timing not isolated.** Few calorie-matched designs with verified, genuinely different eating windows exist [8].
4. **Subgroups.** Older adults, postmenopausal women, people with cardiovascular disease and non-Western or non-white populations lack trials [10][18][8].
5. **Objective adherence.** Most trials depend on self-report [10][8].
6. **Unreplicated observational signal.** The AHA abstract's status remains partly unverified, with two unread PubMed leads [24][25].

Priorities follow directly: long, pragmatic, pre-registered trials with objective intake and timing measurement; calorie-matched designs with distinct windows; individual-participant-data meta-analyses to identify responders; and target-trial-emulation cohort studies to test the mortality signal while controlling for confounding. These are inferences from the gaps, not published roadmaps.

### Forward-looking: GLP-1 receptor agonists

The most plausible near-term development is combination or sequencing with GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide, tirzepatide). A December 2025 narrative review (an expert framework rather than outcome evidence) proposes phased pairing of GLP-1 therapy with structured fasting and protein-optimised nutrition. It notes that the drugs are costly and need ongoing administration, while fasting has minimal direct cost but higher behavioural demands [27]. IMPACT-ADT places TRE and GLP-1 agents in separate arms rather than testing the combination, and results are unpublished (completion date unverified) [21]. This is a projection: whether fasting adds anything on top of a potent pharmacological energy deficit is untested in the retrieved evidence, and it faces the same timing-versus-calories problem.

| Weight | TRE or IF vs no intervention | Small loss; Cochrane "likely little to no difference" [1] | Below usual thresholds | Moderate (Cochrane) [1]; meta-analyses of TRE vs controls show −1.5 to −2.8 kg [17] (conflict) |
| Blood pressure | TRE vs unrestricted eating | ~−1.8 mmHg [3] | Marginal | No GRADE; weight-loss confounding [3] |
| Blood pressure | IF vs CCR | IF less effective for SBP [4] | Unquantified | Unverified (snippet) [4] |
| HbA1c | 5:2 vs CCR in T2D | −0.3% vs −0.5%; equivalence met [5] | Comparable | Single trial, n=137 [5] |
| Glucose/insulin | TRE vs unrestricted | Small improvements [3][18] | Unclear | No GRADE [3][18] |
| LDL cholesterol | ADF vs CR | +11.5 mg/dL with ADF [13] | Adverse direction | Single trial [13] |
| CV events/mortality | Any | No trial data; observational harm signal in short windows [6] | Unknown | Very low; unverified [24][25] |

TRE and IF are reasonable options for adults who find them sustainable, with outcomes broadly comparable to CCR for weight and some markers. The Cochrane authors similarly recommend choosing by "individual practicality and sustainability" [1]. The evidence does not show that fasting is superior, and it cannot establish long-term effects on cardiovascular events. Caution is warranted for people on insulin or sulfonylureas [5][16], and the observational signal for very short windows in people with existing cardiovascular disease is unresolved [6]. These are evidence-grounded considerations, not individual medical advice.
