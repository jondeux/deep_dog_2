## Final Report

# Time-Restricted Eating and Intermittent Fasting vs. Continuous Calorie Restriction: How Strong Is the Evidence for Long-Term Cardiometabolic Benefit in Adults?

## Executive Summary

The evidence does not show that time-restricted eating (TRE) or intermittent fasting (IF) improves long-term cardiometabolic health more than continuous calorie restriction (CCR, also called continuous energy restriction, CER). Where the two are compared directly, they produce broadly similar weight loss and no consistent difference in HbA1c. The one 12-month trial that added TRE to calorie restriction found no significant extra benefit (net difference −1.8 kg, 95% CI −4.0 to 0.4) [1]. A 12-month trial of TRE without calorie counting found a weight loss similar to CCR (difference +0.81 kg, 95% CI −3.07 to 4.69) [2].

The 2025 BMJ network meta-analysis found only alternate-day fasting (ADF) lowered weight more than CER (−1.29 kg, moderate certainty), and the difference was below the authors' 2 kg threshold for minimal importance. In trials of 24 weeks or longer, no IF strategy differed from CER [3].

For cardiovascular events and mortality there is no randomised evidence. The only signal is observational: a US cohort linking eating windows under 8 hours to higher cardiovascular mortality (HR 2.35, 95% CI 1.39–3.98) [4]. It is confounded and exposure-misclassified, so it is hypothesis-generating rather than proof of harm.

Outcome-level judgements below are interpretive syntheses, not formal GRADE ratings unless a source is named.

Conclusions would change with a multi-year pragmatic trial reporting hard endpoints, or with calorie-matched trials showing consistent timing-specific effects.

## Scope, Definitions and Working Thresholds

The aim is to appraise randomised trial and systematic-review evidence on TRE and IF against CCR for adult cardiometabolic outcomes. Terminology varies across the literature. TRE limits eating to a daily window (commonly 4–10 hours) without mandating calorie reduction. IF in the Cochrane and BMJ reviews covers ADF, periodic or whole-day fasting (including 5:2-type regimens), and TRE [3][5]. CCR means a sustained daily energy deficit.

"Long-term" is not defined in the question. The working definition here is 12 months or longer, with 6–12 months treated as medium-term. The Cochrane 2021 review used "short term" for up to 3 months and "medium term" for more than 3 to 12 months [5].

Evidence is drawn from primary trial publications, journal pages of reviews, and one Cochrane press release. Several sources were available only as abstracts or accepted-manuscript previews, and that is noted where it matters. Trials of Trepanowski (2017) ADF and 5:2 trials in type 2 diabetes were not retrieved in this evidence base. Their results are not reported here, and IF-versus-CCR evidence is represented through the meta-analyses.

## Key Randomised Trials

### TRE combined with or compared against CCR

**Liu et al., NEJM 2022** is the largest long-term direct test of TRE against CCR [1]. In Guangzhou, 139 adults with obesity (BMI 28–45, mean age 31.9 years) were randomised for 12 months to 8-hour early TRE (8:00–16:00) plus calorie restriction, or calorie restriction alone. Both arms followed 1,500–1,800 kcal (men) or 1,200–1,500 kcal (women) per day. Weight fell by 8.0 kg with TRE and 6.3 kg with CCR (net −1.8 kg, 95% CI −4.0 to 0.4; P=0.11). Adherence was 84.0% versus 83.8%, and 118 of 139 participants (84.9%) completed [1].

The design matters. Because both arms were calorie-restricted, it tests whether adding a time window helps, not TRE as a substitute for counting calories. The trial excluded diabetes and serious cardiovascular disease and enrolled only Chinese adults. Physical activity and energy expenditure were not objectively controlled, and the confidence intervals were not adjusted for multiplicity [1]. Lean mass fell 1.7 kg versus 1.4 kg (between-group −0.3 kg, 95% CI −1.1 to 0.5), so no excess lean loss was shown [1].

**Lin et al., Annals of Internal Medicine 2023** tested TRE without calorie counting against a prescribed 25% energy deficit and a control [2]. It was a 12-month trial in 90 adults with obesity (BMI 30–50), 33% Black and 46% Hispanic, and 77 completed. Against control, weight loss was −4.61 kg with TRE (95% CI −7.37 to −1.85) and −5.42 kg with CR (95% CI −9.13 to −1.71). TRE versus CR was +0.81 kg (95% CI −3.07 to 4.69; P=0.68) [2].

TRE participants spontaneously cut energy intake by 425 kcal/day versus 405 kcal/day in CR, so the two arms had near-identical energy deficits. This is the clearest evidence that TRE works mainly through reduced intake. The authors acknowledge it was unblinded, not powered for large differences, and unadjusted for multiple comparisons. The trial included a 6-month maintenance phase with a widened 10-hour window, but follow-up beyond 12 months was not reported [2]. Its introduction criticises Liu 2022 for the early window, Chinese-only sample and combined TRE+CR design [2].

**Lowe et al., JAMA Internal Medicine 2020 (TREAT)** is a 12-week trial, not a 12-month one. It randomised 116 adults (BMI 27–43) to 16:8 TRE (noon–8 pm, no calorie counting) or consistent three-meal timing. The weight difference was −0.26 kg (95% CI −1.30 to 0.78; P=0.63) [6]. In the 50-person in-person cohort, appendicular lean mass index was lower with TRE (−0.16 kg/m², 95% CI −0.27 to −0.05) [6]. The comparator was meal timing, not CCR, so it speaks to TRE's independent effect rather than the CCR comparison.

### TRE trials with non-CCR comparators, relevant to the timing question

The **TIMET trial** (Manoogian et al., Annals of Internal Medicine 2024) randomised 122 adults with metabolic syndrome and prediabetes to personalised 8–10-hour TRE or standard care for 3 months [7]. HbA1c fell by a net −0.10% (95% CI −0.19 to −0.003). The authors set a −0.1% change as clinically meaningful in prediabetes [7]. Dietary recall suggested about 350 kcal/day lower intake with TRE, so it was not calorie-matched. The authors report very weak correlations between HbA1c change and weight or calorie change (R² 0.01–0.07), which they offer as evidence of non-weight-mediated effects [7]. Cautions include a primary-outcome switch from fasting glucose to HbA1c (recorded 2 December 2021, with the authors stating no analysis had been run), a baseline age imbalance (56.8 vs 61.4 years), no active control and self-reported diet [7]. The lower confidence bound is almost zero, so the finding is fragile.

The **Dote-Montero trial** (Nature Medicine 2025) randomised 197 adults with overweight or obesity to Mediterranean-diet education alone or with early, late or self-selected 8-hour TRE for 12 weeks. Visceral fat changes did not differ from usual care (early −4%, 95% CI −12 to 4; late −6%, −13 to 2; self-selected −3%, −11 to 5), nor among TRE arms [8]. Only the abstract was available, so secondary outcomes are unverified.

A 2026 **pooled individual-participant analysis** of five myCircadianClock trials (124 adults, 3–6 months) found 8-hour TRE produced 1.66 kg more weight loss than 12-hour windows (95% CI 0.22–3.10) and 2.25 kg more than controls. Each 1-hour shorter window was associated with 0.27 kg additional loss [9]. Associations were attenuated after adjusting for major confounders, and control arms were heterogeneous [9]. It is an accepted-manuscript version that may change.

For weight maintenance, the only post-intervention follow-up retrieved was the **TREHIIT 2-year follow-up** (Scientific Reports 2025). Only 59 of 131 women (about 45%) attended, and 46% of intervention completers self-reported continuing TRE. No between-group differences were significant at the p<0.01 threshold, and adherence was self-reported [10]. It cannot answer the TRE-versus-CCR regain question.

## Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses

### Cochrane reviews

The **2021 Cochrane review** (Allaf et al.; search to 12 December 2019) pooled 18 of 26 eligible RCTs (1,125 adults; 4 weeks to 6 months). No study reported mortality, myocardial infarction, stroke or heart failure. Versus CER, weight change was −0.88 kg short-term (95% CI −1.76 to 0.00; very low certainty) and −0.56 kg medium-term (−1.68 to 0.56; low certainty). Glucose differences were negligible (−0.02 mmol/L, very low certainty). The authors judged the changes clinically insignificant and the evidence low to very low [5].

A **2026 Cochrane update** (CD015610.pub2) was found only through a press release. It reports 22 RCTs and 1,995 adults, mostly up to 12 months of follow-up, and concludes IF is unlikely to produce greater weight loss than traditional dietary advice or no intervention. Side-effect reporting was inconsistent and participants were mostly white and from high-income countries [11]. The release gives no pooled effect sizes, I² or GRADE ratings, and the full text was not read. Its comparator ("traditional dietary advice") differs from CER, so it is not directly comparable with the 2021 review or the BMJ network meta-analysis.

### Network meta-analyses

The **BMJ 2025 network meta-analysis** (Semnani-Azad et al.; search to 14 November 2024) is the most comprehensive comparison. It included 99 RCTs and 6,582 adults, with certainty assessed by CINeMA [3]. Results:

- ADF versus CER: −1.29 kg (95% CI −1.99 to −0.59; moderate certainty).
- TRE versus CER and whole-day fasting versus CER: not significant.
- ADF versus TRE: −1.69 kg; ADF versus whole-day fasting: −1.05 kg.
- HbA1c: no difference for any comparison.
- Trials of 24 weeks or longer (n=17): no IF strategy differed from CER.

Each IF-versus-CER difference was below the 2 kg minimally important threshold the authors set. There were only three direct IF-versus-IF trials and five trials of 52 weeks or more, and an erratum was published in August 2025 whose content was not checked. Several authors disclose food-industry and pharmaceutical ties [3].

The **BMJ Medicine 2026 network meta-analysis** (Chen et al.; search to 3 January 2023) covered TRE only: 41 RCTs, 2,287 participants, interventions over one month. Versus usual diet, overall TRE lowered weight (−2.15 kg), systolic BP (−4.94 mmHg), fat mass and fasting insulin, with no HbA1c or HOMA-IR effect [12]. Against CER, only subtypes were reported: early TRE −1.13 kg (high certainty) and mid TRE −0.91 kg (high). Early TRE beat late TRE by 1.15 kg (95% CI −1.86 to −0.45). TRE under 8 hours lowered fat mass but raised total cholesterol by 14.47 mg/dL. The authors caution that CINeMA ratings may be optimistic and direct evidence is scarce [12]. Fat-free mass fell 0.84 kg versus usual diet [12].

### Umbrella review and pairwise meta-analyses

The **eClinicalMedicine 2024 umbrella review** (Sun et al.) synthesised 23 meta-analyses, 351 associations and 34 outcomes, with 91% of the meta-analyses rated high confidence on AMSTAR. Of 103 nominally significant associations, only 10 (10%) had high GRADE certainty [13]. Against CER, twice-weekly fasting reduced weight by 1.37 kg (95% CI −2.24 to −0.49; moderate certainty). Systolic BP was higher with IF than CER (SMD 0.21, 95% CI 0.05–0.36; high certainty) [13]. Overlap of primary trials across the included meta-analyses was not quantified, so the high-certainty count may double-count trials [13].

The **Frontiers 2025 meta-analysis** (Yi et al.) pooled 11 RCTs (653 non-diabetic adults) of TRE without calorie restriction against unrestricted eating. Systolic BP fell 1.79 mmHg (95% CI −3.30 to −0.27), fasting glucose 2.65 mg/dL and HOMA-IR 0.58, with no lipid effect [14]. Eight of 11 trials had "some concerns" and three "high" risk of bias. The weight loss in "isocaloric" arms means calorie matching was not maintained, so timing cannot be separated from energy deficit [14]. Some of the same small trials, including Lowe 2020 and Lin 2023, appear in several reviews, so these syntheses are not fully independent.

### Why the reviews disagree

The reviews conflict on whether TRE beats CER. The BMJ 2025 network meta-analysis finds no difference [3], and the BMJ Medicine 2026 network meta-analysis finds early and mid TRE modestly better [12]. The umbrella review finds an advantage for twice-weekly fasting where the BMJ network finds none for whole-day fasting [13][3].

Plausible drivers are differences in how regimens are split into subtypes, minimum intervention length (over one month in the BMJ Medicine review), search dates, and comparator definitions. The BMJ Medicine review's search predates the BMJ 2025 review by almost two years. Source-based reasoning supports treating the larger, later network (99 trials, with a trial-duration sensitivity analysis) as the more conservative anchor. Even the more favourable estimates are around 1 kg, and the BMJ 2025 authors treat differences under 2 kg as not clinically important [3].

## Outcome-by-Outcome Assessment

### Body weight and adiposity

Weight is the best-evidenced outcome. All IF strategies and CER beat ad libitum eating, but versus CER the differences are small. The 12-month trials show no significant TRE-versus-CCR difference [1][2]. The network meta-analysis shows a small ADF advantage in shorter trials, which disappears at 24 weeks or more [3]. For adiposity, Liu 2022 found fat-mass differences that did not reach significance (−1.5 kg, 95% CI −3.1 to 0.2) [1], and the umbrella review reports a fat-mass advantage for twice-weekly fasting of 0.82 kg [13].

Interpretation: similar weight loss, with intake falling similarly in calorie-unmatched trials [2], supports an energy-deficit explanation. Certainty is moderate for short-to-medium-term equivalence and low beyond 12 months. Weight regain after intervention is essentially unevidenced for TRE versus CCR in the sources reviewed [10].

### Blood pressure

Direct TRE-versus-CCR evidence is limited. Liu 2022 found a systolic BP difference of −0.3 mmHg (95% CI −3.7 to 3.1) [1]. Against non-CCR comparators, estimates diverge. The BMJ Medicine network meta-analysis reports −4.94 mmHg versus usual diet (95% CI −6.92 to −2.95) [12], while the Frontiers meta-analysis of TRE without calorie restriction reports −1.79 mmHg (−3.30 to −0.27), with benefit concentrated in participants with high baseline BP [14].

The umbrella review points the other way against CER: IF was less effective at lowering systolic BP (SMD 0.21, high certainty) [13]. Taken together, there is no evidence that TRE or IF lowers BP more than CCR, and some evidence it is weaker. Ambulatory-versus-clinic measurement and weight-mediation analyses were not found in the retrieved sources.

### Glycaemic control

HbA1c shows no consistent advantage. The BMJ 2025 network meta-analysis found no HbA1c difference across IF strategies, CER and ad libitum diets [3]. The BMJ Medicine network meta-analysis found no TRE effect on HbA1c or HOMA-IR, with I² above 90% [12]. Cochrane 2021 found glucose changes of about 0.02 mmol/L versus CER [5]. The favourable signals come from non-CCR comparisons: TIMET's −0.10% HbA1c [7] and the Frontiers meta-analysis showing lower fasting glucose and HOMA-IR [14]. These are small and short, and the Frontiers review excluded diabetes entirely [14].

Hypoglycaemia and medication de-escalation in diabetes, including sulfonylurea or insulin adjustment, could not be assessed from the retrieved sources. The Cochrane 2021 authors explicitly flag diabetes as an area needing further research [5].

### Cardiovascular events and mortality

No randomised evidence exists. Cochrane 2021 found no trial reporting mortality, myocardial infarction, stroke or heart failure [5]. The BMJ Medicine network meta-analysis found none with hard endpoints, with the longest trial about one year [12]. The 2025 cohort paper states that long-term RCTs on hard endpoints are unavailable and will be challenging to conduct [4].

The observational signal comes from Chen, Zhong and colleagues. The March 2024 American Heart Association abstract analysed 20,078 NHANES adults (2003–2018, mortality to December 2019). Eating windows under 8 hours were linked to cardiovascular mortality (HR 1.91, 95% CI 1.20–3.03) against a 12–16-hour reference, with no significant all-cause association (HR 1.26, 0.91–1.74). Only 414 participants had windows under 8 hours [15]. The peer-reviewed version (Diabetes & Metabolic Syndrome, 2025) used 19,831 adults and a 12–14-hour reference, with a median 8.1 years of follow-up. It reports an HR of 2.35 (95% CI 1.39–3.98), with no association with all-cause or cancer mortality. The result survived 8 subgroup and 14 sensitivity analyses, while the all-cause association did not [4]. The shift in estimates between abstract and paper reflects changes in sample and reference group. The paper's authors state that residual confounding versus a true effect remains unresolved [4].

The competing readings are clear. Concerned readers see a possible harm signal that survived sensitivity testing, a view supported by co-author Zhong, who advised "extreme" caution about following TRE for years and suggested lower lean muscle mass as a pathway [16]. Sceptics note the following:

- Exposure was classified from two 24-hour recalls, which Varady called "not at all reflective" of habitual eating, and Spiegelhalter questioned whether out-of-window food was simply unrecorded [17][16].
- The short-window group had more men, Black participants, current smokers and food-insecure people, and shift work, stress and diet quality were not captured [4][16].
- Reverse causation is plausible: people worried about heart health may restrict eating [17].
- Sanders suggested shift workers might be over-represented [17].
- The abstract was initially unreviewed, and the AHA later regretted that some media coverage lacked context [16].

Methodologically, the sceptical reading is better supported. Exposure misclassification, unmeasured confounding and the absence of a causal design all bias toward spurious associations, and even the peer-reviewed paper stops short of causal language [4]. The signal should prompt trials, not clinical alarm, and co-author Manson herself said the findings should not lead to alarm or dietary changes [16]. A 2025 commentary in the same journal, titled "Short-term 24h dietary recalls from observational studies cannot support claims on mortality," was identified only by title in a citing-articles list and its content was not reviewed [4].

## Robustness of the Evidence

### Calorie and weight-loss matching

Calorie matching is the central interpretive problem. The few calorie-matched or isocaloric tests are small and short, and the Frontiers review notes participants in "isocaloric" arms still lost weight, so matching was not maintained [14]. Of the calorie-unmatched TRE trials, TIMET and Lin 2023 show intake falling by roughly 350–425 kcal/day [7][2]. Intake in these trials was measured by recall or logging, not doubly labelled water.

Two readings follow. If similar weight loss means similar energy deficit, TRE is a different route to the same mechanism, as Lin 2023 suggests [2]. If outcomes such as HbA1c diverge from weight, as TIMET's weak weight-HbA1c correlation implies, timing effects remain possible [7]. The pooled 8-hour-versus-12-hour result is consistent with a dose-response for window length, but it was attenuated after adjustment [9]. The evidence does not resolve the question.

### Risk of bias, adherence and attrition

Blinding of participants is impossible, which matters for self-reported intake and adherence. In the Frontiers review, 8 of 11 trials had "some concerns" and 3 "high" risk, with non-adherence the most common problem [14]. The BMJ Medicine review rated about 90% of trials low risk of bias, a more favourable assessment, but its authors caution that CINeMA ratings may be optimistic [12]. These two reviews assess overlapping trial types and diverge sharply, which reflects judgement differences.

Adherence and attrition in the long trials were reasonable: 84.9% completion in Liu 2022 [1] and 86% in Lin 2023 [2]. These were intensively supported trials with dietitian or coach contact, so real-world adherence is probably lower. The longest follow-up retrieved had about 55% attrition [10].

### Trial duration versus the long-term horizon

The evidence is mostly short. The BMJ Medicine network meta-analysis had a median intervention of 8 weeks (range 4–48 weeks) [12]. Cochrane 2021 covered 4 weeks to 6 months [5]. The BMJ 2025 network meta-analysis had 17 trials of 24 weeks or longer and only five of 52 weeks or longer [3]. Only two TRE-versus-CCR trials reached 12 months [1][2], and none beyond. Short-term weight and metabolic changes cannot be extrapolated to cardiovascular outcomes, and the findings say nothing about multi-year maintenance.

### Surrogates, funding and publication bias

Every outcome except the observational mortality data is a surrogate (weight, BP, HbA1c, lipids). The umbrella review found that fewer than 1 in 10 significant associations had high-certainty support, and 42% of associations showed high heterogeneity [13]. Sample size and follow-up needed for a hard-endpoint trial were not quantified in the retrieved sources, and no calculation is offered here.

On funding, Liu 2022 was publicly funded [1], TIMET had NIH funding [7], Cochrane 2021 notes two studies funded by diet-food companies [5], and the BMJ 2025 authors disclose food-industry and pharmaceutical ties [3]. Several investigators, including Varady, appear across trial, umbrella-review and commentary roles [2][13][16], and the pooled analysis [9] and TIMET [7] come from the same research group. No formal conflict conclusions can be drawn from the retrieved text. Funnel-plot and Egger assessments, and registered-but-unpublished trials, were not retrieved.

## Safety and Tolerability

Lean mass is the main safety signal, and it is inconsistent. Lowe 2020 found lower appendicular lean mass index with TRE [6]. Liu 2022 found no between-arm difference [1]. TIMET reported lean-mass loss of 0.28 kg of 2.98 kg total weight loss, described as favourable [7], and the full text says no greater lean decrease than standard care [7]. The BMJ Medicine network meta-analysis found fat-free mass fell 0.84 kg (95% CI −1.27 to −0.41) versus usual diet and recommends resistance training [12]. Lean mass fell in both TRE and CCR arms in Liu 2022, so the issue is not TRE-specific.

Adverse events were generally mild: no serious events in 14 trials with data, with mild dizziness, headache and constipation [12], and mild headaches in Cochrane 2021 [5]. The 2026 Cochrane update notes inconsistent reporting [11]. Hypoglycaemia in diabetes, eating-disorder risk, bone health, gallstones, menstrual or fertility effects and sleep were not reported in the retrieved sources [12]. Populations with diabetes, cardiovascular disease and eating disorders are poorly covered. Liu 2022 and Lin 2023 excluded diabetes, and Lin also excluded night-shift workers [1][2].

## Subgroup Differences

Subgroup evidence is sparse and mostly post hoc. BP benefit appears concentrated in people with elevated baseline BP [14]. TIMET's glycaemic effect applies to adults with metabolic syndrome and prediabetes [7]. Early TRE outperformed late TRE in the BMJ Medicine network meta-analysis [12], but the Dote-Montero trial found no difference among early, late and self-selected windows for visceral fat [8]. Liu 2022 found similar weight changes across sex, BMI, insulin sensitivity and adherence subgroups [1]. Lin 2023 enrolled a racially diverse sample but reported no subgroup differences in the retrieved text [2]. The 2026 Cochrane update notes most participants were white and from high-income countries [11].

Evidence on sex, older adults, postmenopausal women, shift workers and established cardiovascular disease was not retrieved. Some narrative reviews in 2025–2026 reference sex-specific responses, but their content was not reviewed. These remain gaps.

## Where the Evidence Is Weakest or Contradictory

The weaknesses, ranked by consequence for the question:

1. **No hard-endpoint RCT.** No trial reports cardiovascular events or mortality [5][12][4].
2. **Scarcity of trials beyond 12 months.** Only five 52-week-plus trials exist in the largest network [3], and only two TRE-versus-CCR trials reached 12 months [1][2].
3. **Meta-analytic disagreement.** The BMJ 2025 and BMJ Medicine 2026 networks disagree on TRE versus CER, and the umbrella review disagrees with the BMJ network on whole-day fasting [3][12][13]. Review-level conflicts are driven by classification and search dates, not new trials.
4. **Small samples and wide intervals.** Liu 2022's interval allows a difference from −4.0 to +0.4 kg [1], and Lin 2023's allows −3.07 to +4.69 kg [2]. Neither trial can exclude a clinically relevant difference in either direction.
5. **Observational versus trial conflict on cardiovascular risk.** The NHANES signal sits against short-term favourable surrogate trends [4][14].
6. **Calorie-matching ambiguity.** Whether benefit exists independent of weight loss is unresolved [7][14].
7. **Heterogeneous protocols.** Window length, timing, calorie targets and support vary widely [12][13].

A non-obvious pattern emerges. TRE appears to perform similarly to CCR and may be easier for some people because it removes calorie counting, a feasibility argument raised in the trial literature [2]. However, Liu 2022 found equal adherence under intensive support [1], so the feasibility advantage is asserted more than shown. A second irony: the umbrella review gives "high certainty" to IF being worse than CER for systolic BP, while TRE-versus-usual-diet reviews report BP gains. Whether a regimen "improves BP" depends entirely on the comparator.

## Open Research Gaps and Future Directions

Needed studies include pragmatic multi-year trials of TRE or IF against CCR with objective intake and timing measurement, trials with cardiovascular endpoints or validated intermediate imaging, trials in type 2 diabetes with medication-adjustment outcomes, and trials in older adults, women across menopause and shift workers. Methodological needs include standardised protocol reporting, objective energy-expenditure measurement and core outcome sets. Several newly published items surfaced only as titles (a 2025 post-intervention TRE versus caloric restriction secondary analysis and a 2026 TEMPUS trial of TRE with exercise on liver fat) and were not reviewed [8][9].

Registry searches (ClinicalTrials.gov, ISRCTN, ANZCTR, WHO ICTRP) for ongoing 12-month-plus or cardiovascular-endpoint trials were not completed. No registered completion dates can be offered, and forward-looking statements here are projections: given the dominance of short trials and funding trends, the near-term evidence base will most likely grow in surrogate-outcome trials, not hard-endpoint trials. A full-text reading of the 2026 Cochrane update, which should report pooled estimates and GRADE ratings, would most improve the evidence summary.

## Conclusions and Practical Implications for Evidence Interpretation

| Outcome | Best available evidence | Direction and size vs. CCR | Certainty | Key limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Weight/adiposity | BMJ 2025 NMA; Liu 2022; Lin 2023 [3][1][2] | Similar; ADF about −1.3 kg in shorter trials, gone at ≥24 wk | Moderate (ADF, short-term) [3]; low beyond 12 mo (assessment) | Few trials >12 mo; calorie matching |
| Blood pressure | Liu 2022; umbrella review; TRE meta-analyses [1][13][14][12] | No advantage; IF possibly weaker (SMD 0.21) | High for IF weaker (umbrella) [13]; otherwise low (assessment) | Divergent estimates by comparator |
| Glycaemic control | BMJ 2025 NMA; Cochrane 2021; TIMET [3][5][7] | No HbA1c difference | Moderate to very low [5] | Short trials; few with diabetes |
| CV events/mortality | NHANES cohort [4] | Unknown; observational HR 2.35 for <8 h vs 12–14 h | No RCT evidence; observational only | Confounding; two-day recall |

What is reasonably established is that TRE and IF produce short-to-medium-term weight loss comparable to CCR, with at most small differences that fall below the BMJ authors' 2 kg importance threshold [3]. Uncertain or unsupported are long-term cardiovascular benefit or harm, mortality effects, timing-specific benefit independent of energy intake, and safety in diabetes and other high-risk groups. This is an evidence appraisal, not clinical advice. Where TRE or IF suits a person's preferences, the current evidence supports treating it as one of several ways to achieve an energy deficit, not as a demonstrably superior one.
